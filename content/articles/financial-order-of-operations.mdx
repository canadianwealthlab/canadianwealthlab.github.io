## Executive Summary

There is no universal financial order of operations. A defensible default is to keep required obligations current, build a starter cash buffer, capture unusually valuable employer benefits, eliminate expensive debt, establish an adequate reserve, and then fund goals through the accounts and investments that fit their timing and tax treatment. Mortgage prepayment and taxable investing enter after liquidity, debt cost, taxes, expected returns, and risk are compared on the same basis.

Sequence changes when the facts change. A household facing job uncertainty may hold more cash. Someone with credit-card debt may prioritize the guaranteed interest saving. An eligible first-time buyer may value FHSA room. A high-income worker expecting a lower future tax rate may favour RRSP contributions. A person with a short home-purchase horizon may use stable savings even when long-run equity returns are higher.

## Define the Decision

The question is not which financial product is best. It is which use of a limited dollar creates the greatest improvement after considering certainty, liquidity, tax, time horizon, fees, risk, and consequences of being wrong.

Use this comparison for every option:

> Expected net benefit = certain savings or expected return minus tax minus fees minus the value of lost flexibility.

Expected investment return is uncertain. Interest avoided by repaying debt is generally known, subject to contract terms. Employer matching may be highly valuable but can have vesting or access restrictions. Registered accounts change tax timing but do not determine investment risk.

## Step 1: Keep Obligations Current

Pay housing costs, utilities, tax instalments where required, minimum debt payments, and other essential commitments before optimizing. The cost of missing an obligation can include penalties, higher rates, loss of service, forced asset sales, or damaged credit.

If cash flow is negative, the next dollar may need to remain in the operating account while fixed commitments are reviewed. Investing while repeatedly borrowing to cover ordinary expenses simply moves risk to a more expensive balance sheet.

## Step 2: Build a Starter Buffer

A starter buffer covers predictable friction: a deductible, urgent repair, irregular bill, or a gap between income and a payment date. It is smaller than a complete emergency reserve. The right amount depends on the size and timing of likely shocks, not a universal number.

Example: a renter with two stable incomes, no vehicle, and low fixed costs may need less initial cash than a single-income homeowner with an older vehicle and variable commissions. Both may eventually target several months of essential expenses, but the second household has a larger cost of being short of cash.

## Step 3: Evaluate Employer Matching

An employer contribution can change the economics. If a plan adds $1 for each $1 contributed up to a limit, an employee contribution may receive an immediate 100% gross uplift before investment returns. That does not make every plan contribution automatically first priority. Confirm eligibility, vesting, withdrawal restrictions, fees, investment choices, and what happens when employment ends.

For a worker with high-cost overdue debt or no ability to meet essential bills, stabilizing cash flow can still be more urgent. For a stable household, contributing enough to capture a match while addressing other priorities may be reasonable.

## Step 4: Repay Expensive Debt

Debt repayment produces a return equal to interest avoided, adjusted for any tax treatment and prepayment cost. Consumer interest is generally paid with after-tax dollars and is not deductible. A 19.99% credit-card balance therefore sets a high hurdle for investing.

Worked example: applying $10,000 to a 12% line of credit avoids about $1,200 of first-year interest if the balance would otherwise remain constant. A taxable investment earning 7% would produce $700 before tax and fees, with a risk of loss. The debt decision is stronger on both expected value and certainty, although maintaining necessary liquidity still matters.

At lower rates the comparison narrows. Do not group a 3% student loan, a 5% mortgage, and a 20% credit card under one generic rule.

## Step 5: Complete the Emergency Reserve

Estimate essential monthly outflows, then choose a range based on risk.

> Reserve target = essential monthly outflows × months of protection.

If essential outflows are $4,500 and the chosen range is three to six months, the target is $13,500 to $27,000. That range is a planning decision. It should be increased for unstable income, a single earner, concentrated employment risk, near-term leave, or large property obligations. It may be reduced when several stable income sources and other reliable liquid resources exist.

Keep emergency money accessible and stable. The objective is availability, not maximum return.

## Step 6: Check FHSA Eligibility and Near-Term Goals

For an eligible first-time home buyer, the FHSA may offer deductible contributions and tax-free qualifying withdrawals. In 2026, the annual participation limit is $8,000 and the lifetime limit is $40,000. Opening an account starts the participation-period rules, so timing matters. Confirm eligibility, room, qualifying-withdrawal requirements, and the maximum participation period with CRA guidance.

Money for a home purchase within a few years should not automatically be invested in equities simply because it is inside an FHSA. Account tax treatment and investment risk are separate decisions.

## Step 7: Compare TFSA and RRSP Use

A TFSA uses after-tax contributions and generally allows tax-free growth and withdrawals. An RRSP can provide a deduction and tax-deferred growth, but withdrawals are generally taxable and room is not restored. The RRSP refund is the return of tax that would otherwise have been collected on the deducted income. It is not a bonus independent of future tax.

A simplified RRSP contribution estimate is:

> Current tax reduction = deductible contribution × current marginal tax rate.

A $10,000 deduction at a 40% marginal rate may reduce current tax by about $4,000 before other interactions. If the $10,000 contribution and $4,000 tax reduction are both invested, the RRSP comparison is economically different from spending the refund. Future withdrawal tax, benefit clawbacks, contribution room, flexibility, and the tax rate at deduction versus withdrawal all matter.

TFSA use can be attractive for flexible goals, lower current tax rates, and withdrawals that should not add to taxable income. RRSP use can be attractive when deductions are valuable now, retirement is the main goal, and future withdrawal rates are expected to be lower. These are tendencies, not guarantees.

## Step 8: Compare Mortgage Prepayment and Investing

Mortgage prepayment offers a certain interest saving and lower future required debt. Investing offers liquidity before retirement-account restrictions and a higher expected long-run return, with volatility and possible loss.

For a non-deductible mortgage at 5%, a prepayment has an approximate 5% after-tax guaranteed return before considering penalties and lost liquidity. A taxable interest investment would need a higher pre-tax return to match it. An equity portfolio may have a higher expected return over a long horizon, but the realized outcome can be much lower, especially over short periods.

Test both cases using the same period. Include mortgage privileges, renewal risk, account tax treatment, investment fees, and whether the household will actually remain invested during a decline.

## Step 9: Use Taxable Investing Deliberately

Taxable investing becomes relevant when registered room is unavailable, unsuitable for the goal, or intentionally reserved. It adds recordkeeping and tax considerations: interest, Canadian dividends, foreign income, capital gains, adjusted cost base, return of capital, and foreign withholding can receive different treatment.

The absence of registered room does not make a complex portfolio necessary. A simple diversified investment can remain appropriate, but account location and recordkeeping should be planned before transactions accumulate.

## Three Worked Scenarios

### Stable professional with a match and credit-card debt

Assume $1,200 per month is available, a full employer match requires $300, credit-card debt costs 20%, and the household has only $1,000 cash. One reasonable sequence is to build a modest starter buffer, contribute enough to capture the match, direct most remaining cash to the card, then build the full reserve. Investing beyond the match is hard to justify while the 20% balance remains.

### First-time buyer with no expensive debt

Assume stable cash flow, a four-month reserve, a purchase goal in three years, and FHSA eligibility. FHSA contributions may be prioritized, but the investments inside should reflect the short horizon. TFSA savings may support flexibility. An RRSP deduction could still be useful, but using the Home Buyers' Plan creates a repayment obligation and should be compared with preserving retirement assets.

### Homeowner choosing between mortgage and investing

Assume a 5% mortgage, 15-year horizon, adequate reserves, no expensive debt, and available TFSA room. Prepayment offers a certain 5% saving and less renewal exposure. TFSA investing may offer a higher expected return and more accessible assets, but outcomes vary. Splitting the dollar can be rational when both debt reduction and long-term asset growth matter.

## Decision Framework

1. Is any required obligation at risk?
2. Would using the money leave inadequate operating cash?
3. Is there an employer benefit with unusually favourable terms?
4. What certain after-tax interest cost can be avoided?
5. When will the money be needed, and how much loss can the plan tolerate?
6. Which registered account is available and appropriate?
7. What taxes, fees, restrictions, and lost liquidity apply?
8. What happens if returns disappoint, income falls, or the goal arrives early?

Choose the option that remains acceptable across several reasonable scenarios, not only the one with the highest optimistic outcome.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating all debt as equally urgent.
- Counting a tax refund without investing or otherwise assigning it.
- Using equity returns as if they were guaranteed debt-comparison rates.
- Contributing without verifying personal TFSA, RRSP, or FHSA room.
- Re-contributing a TFSA withdrawal in the same year without available room.
- Locking every dollar into debt repayment and leaving no liquidity.
- Holding a near-term down payment in an investment that can fall sharply.

## FAQ

### Should I always take an employer match before paying debt?

No. Compare the match terms with the urgency and cost of the debt, vesting, access restrictions, and the household's ability to meet essential obligations.

### Is mortgage prepayment always equivalent to a guaranteed return?

It generally avoids contractual interest, but prepayment limits, penalties, tax treatment, and lost liquidity can affect the result. Confirm the mortgage terms.

### When should taxable investing begin?

When it fits the goal and overall plan, often after suitable registered opportunities are considered. Registered room does not have to be exhausted if its restrictions or tax treatment do not fit the purpose.
