## Executive Summary

Rebalancing restores the intended risk level; it is not a return forecast. Use calendar review, percentage bands, or both. Directing contributions and withdrawals can reduce trades and tax. During a decline, first confirm near-term cash needs, then compare current allocation with the written policy. Change the plan only if the goal or risk capacity changed.

## Why Portfolios Drift

Assets earn different returns. A 60% equity allocation can become 70% after equities outperform, increasing risk. After a decline it can fall below target, reducing expected growth. Rebalancing sells or buys to restore the planned range.

Example: a $500,000 portfolio targets 60% equity and 40% fixed income. After market movement it holds $330,000 equity and $170,000 fixed income, or 66% and 34%. Returning to target requires $30,000 shifted from equity to fixed income, before considering new cash, tax, and fees.

## Choose a Rule

A calendar rule reviews quarterly or annually. A threshold rule acts when an asset differs by a set number of percentage points or proportion from target. Very narrow bands create unnecessary trading; very wide bands allow risk to change materially.

Write the rule before volatility. Apply it consistently rather than using the threshold only when a trade feels comfortable.

## Use Cash Flows First

Direct new contributions to underweight assets and fund withdrawals from overweight ones. In taxable accounts, consider realized gains and losses. Rebalancing across household accounts requires respecting each account's tax and withdrawal rules.

Do not let tax avoidance create an allocation the plan cannot tolerate. Sometimes accepting a taxable gain is the cost of reducing excessive risk.

## During a Decline

1. Confirm operating and near-term spending cash.
2. Recheck the goal and withdrawal date.
3. Measure allocation, do not rely on headlines.
4. Follow the contribution and rebalancing policy.
5. Avoid leverage or concentrated “recovery” trades.
6. Change risk only if the original assessment was wrong or circumstances changed.

Selling after a decline can be appropriate when money was misclassified as long-term, the portfolio exceeded capacity, or the investment was unsuitable. “Never sell” is as unhelpful as “get out before it gets worse.”

## Sequence Risk for Retirees

Retirees withdrawing during a decline can permanently impair the portfolio. Stable near-term assets, flexible discretionary spending, and planned rebalancing can reduce forced sales. Holding excessive cash has an expected-return and inflation cost, so define the amount by withdrawals rather than fear.

## Common Mistakes

- Rebalancing based on forecasts.
- Checking only one account rather than total allocation.
- Ignoring tax and trading costs.
- Setting an allocation that cannot survive a normal severe decline.
- Stopping contributions because prices fell without a cash-flow reason.
- Buying a concentrated asset to recover faster.

## FAQ

### How often should I rebalance?

There is no universal frequency. Annual review with sensible bands is common, but portfolio size, cash flows, tax, and product structure matter.

### Should I buy more after a crash?

Follow the existing allocation rule. Do not increase risk simply because prices fell unless the policy and capacity support it.

### Can an all-in-one fund rebalance automatically?

Many asset-allocation funds do, according to their mandate. The investor still must confirm the fund allocation remains appropriate.

## Expected Returns Are Assumptions

Expected returns support planning; they do not predict the next year. Build them from the asset allocation, subtract fees, reflect tax where relevant, and use a range. A plan that works only with an optimistic return needs more saving, time, spending flexibility, or a different goal.

Historical data provides context but depends on market, period, currency, inflation, and survivorship. Current yields and valuations can inform long-run assumptions without forecasting short-term outcomes.

## Nominal, Real, and Net Returns

Nominal returns include inflation. Real returns describe purchasing-power growth.

> Real return = (1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation) - 1.

At 5% nominal and 2% inflation, real return is about 2.94%. If fund and advice costs total 1.25%, subtract them from the gross investment assumption before tax. A taxable account, TFSA, and RRSP do not produce identical spendable outcomes.

## Worked Planning Range

If $15,000 is contributed at each year-end for 25 years, hypothetical returns of 3%, 5%, and 7% produce about $547,000, $716,000, and $949,000. The $402,000 spread is assumption risk. Actual annual results are uneven and can create different outcomes even with the same average.

Do not increase the return assumption solely to close a shortfall. That changes the risk description, not the savings available.

## Connect Returns to Rebalancing

Rebalancing realizes the policy response to different asset returns. It does not depend on knowing which asset will perform next. If equities exceed the permitted range after strong returns, reducing them restores risk. If they fall below the range, contributions or trades restore exposure only when liquidity and capacity remain intact.

Tax can justify using wider bands or cash flows, but should not allow an unsuitable allocation indefinitely.

## Projection Checklist

1. Start with the target allocation.
2. Select documented gross return ranges.
3. Subtract fund, advice, trading, and other recurring costs.
4. Model tax by account and income type.
5. Convert nominal and real dollars consistently.
6. Test a poor early sequence and higher inflation.
7. Use the policy rebalancing rule, not a forecast.
8. Record the assumption source and review date.

## Return Assumption Mistakes

- Using recent returns as the planning rate.
- Ignoring fees, tax, and inflation.
- Applying one return to every asset and account.
- Treating a smooth average as a realistic path.
- Raising expected returns to repair an underfunded plan.
- Reporting precise dollar outcomes without sensitivity analysis.
