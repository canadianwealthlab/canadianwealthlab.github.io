## Quick Answer

Start with the annual spending your portfolio must fund, subtract reliable income such as CPP, OAS, or a pension, then divide the remaining amount by a cautious withdrawal rate. A simple 4% rule implies 25 times annual portfolio-funded spending, but taxes, retirement length, asset mix, flexibility, and benefit timing can move the result materially.

## The Decision

A retirement target is not a universal number. It is the price of a particular lifestyle funded under a particular set of assumptions. Two households with identical portfolios may need very different amounts because their spending, housing, pensions, taxes, and willingness to adjust differ.

The goal is a planning range that can be updated—not false precision decades in advance.

## Key Factors

### Spending, not salary

Retirement planning begins with expected expenses. Work costs may fall, travel or health costs may rise, and a paid-off home can change the baseline. Separate essential from flexible spending.

### Income outside the portfolio

CPP, OAS, workplace pensions, annuities, part-time work, and rental income can reduce the amount investments must support. Account for timing and tax rather than simply subtracting gross annual estimates.

### Withdrawal rate and retirement length

A lower initial withdrawal rate generally requires more capital but provides a larger margin for poor markets, longevity, and unexpected costs. Retiring very early can require a more conservative plan.

## Scenario Analysis

### Traditional retirement with pension income

Reliable pension income can cover a meaningful share of core spending. The portfolio may primarily fund discretionary goals, inflation gaps, and contingencies.

### Early retirement

An early retiree has more years to fund and may wait longer for public benefits. Sequence-of-returns risk matters more, and the target may need additional margin.

### Flexible spending

A household willing to reduce discretionary withdrawals after weak markets may be able to use a different planning range than one with inflexible obligations.

## Common Mistakes

- Multiplying gross salary by an arbitrary percentage.
- Ignoring taxes on RRSP and RRIF withdrawals.
- Treating home equity as spendable without a plan to access it.
- Using one return forecast with no downside scenario.
- Forgetting one-time costs and long-term care risk.

## Decision Framework

Estimate annual essential and discretionary spending in today’s dollars. Map expected income sources by age and after tax. Calculate the portfolio gap, test several withdrawal rates, and model weak early returns. Update the range annually and after major life changes.

## FAQ

### Is the 4% rule safe in Canada?

It is a historical planning rule, not a guarantee. Fees, taxes, asset mix, retirement length, and future returns can all change the result.

### Should my home count toward my retirement number?

Only if you have a realistic plan to sell, downsize, borrow against it, or otherwise convert equity into spendable resources.

### Do CPP and OAS reduce what I need?

Yes, when eligible benefits cover part of future spending. Model the start dates, taxes, and possible benefit changes carefully.
