## Quick Answer

> **Key takeaway:** High-rate consumer debt usually comes before investing because the interest avoided is certain, while investment returns are uncertain. Low-rate debt creates a closer decision.

Before choosing either option, keep essential payments current and preserve adequate emergency savings. Then compare the same dollar over the same period, after investment fees and tax.

- **Lean toward debt repayment** when the rate is high or variable, cash flow is strained, the investment horizon is short, or lower fixed obligations matter most.
- **Lean toward investing** when the debt rate is low, the horizon is long, liquidity is adequate, and valuable employer matching or registered-account opportunities are available.
- **Consider a split** when neither option clearly dominates and both certainty and long-term asset growth have meaningful value.

## Compare Debt Repayment and Investing

The central comparison is a predictable financing cost against an uncertain investment outcome. Put both rates on the same basis before deciding.

> **Personal non-deductible debt:** return from repayment is approximately the interest rate avoided.

> **Taxable investment:** net return equals gross return minus fees and applicable tax.

| Consideration | Paying down debt | Investing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Return | Interest avoided is generally predictable when no penalty applies. | Returns vary and can be negative. |
| Best fit | Higher or variable rates, shorter horizons, or a need to reduce obligations. | Lower debt rates, longer horizons, adequate liquidity, and suitable risk tolerance. |
| Liquidity | Repayment can be difficult to reverse. Available credit is not guaranteed access on the same terms. | Investments may be accessible, but their value can be lower when needed. RRSP withdrawals add tax and lost room. |
| Tax treatment | Personal interest is usually compared on an after-tax basis. Deductibility is fact-specific and should not be assumed. | TFSA growth and withdrawals are generally tax-free. RRSP deductions and taxable withdrawals must be considered together. |
| Main risk | Using too much cash can force new borrowing after an emergency. | Market losses, fees, tax, and investor behaviour can reduce the expected benefit. |

### When debt repayment is stronger

- The rate is high or variable.
- Cash flow is strained or recurring expenses are returning to credit.
- The investment horizon is short.
- Lower fixed obligations would improve household resilience.
- A rate increase would materially weaken the plan.

The benefit may be smaller when prepayment charges apply, interest is deductible in a valid income-earning context, or repayment consumes cash needed for emergencies.

### When investing is stronger

- The debt rate is low and fixed.
- The investment horizon is long.
- Emergency and near-term goal money is already protected.
- The asset mix is appropriate for the goal and the investor can remain invested through a decline.
- Employer matching or registered-account value materially improves the investing case.

Expected return is not a promise. Borrowing at 5% while holding an investment after a 30% loss can feel very different from comparing long-term averages on paper.

### Employer matching and registered accounts

An employer match can create an immediate benefit that changes the sequence. Confirm vesting, plan costs, access, and eligible contribution limits.

A TFSA can protect investment growth and withdrawals from tax, but contributions are not deductible. An RRSP may create a deduction, but future withdrawals are taxable. The RRSP comparison should include the use of the tax reduction because spending it weakens the long-term case.

## See How the Debt Rate Changes the Decision

| Example | What the comparison suggests | What could change it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Credit card at 20% | With a starter reserve and no employer match, repayment is usually the dominant financial case. A prudent investment cannot reliably match the certain 20% saving. | An immediate employer match may justify limited parallel investing. |
| Line of credit at 8% | Repayment remains compelling, especially if the rate floats. | A match may justify parallel investing. A split can preserve the investing habit without minimizing the debt cost. |
| Fixed loan at 3% | With a long horizon, strong cash flow, and suitable registered room, investing can have a higher expected outcome. | Repayment still provides certainty and lower obligations. Test a return range and a major early decline. |

> **A simple $10,000 comparison:** paying $10,000 toward 18% debt avoids about $1,800 of first-year interest if the balance would otherwise remain unchanged. A 7% investment earns $700 before fees and tax in the same simplified year, and it can lose value. At a 4% loan rate, the saving is about $400, making liquidity and tax treatment more important to the decision.

## Protect Liquidity Before Optimizing

Keep emergency and near-term goal money outside this comparison. A mathematically attractive choice can be poor if it forces later borrowing.

Debt repayment can be difficult to reverse. A paid credit card creates available credit, but not guaranteed access on the same terms.

An investment account may be liquid, but market value can be lower when the money is needed. RRSP withdrawals also create tax and permanently use contribution room.

## A Simple Decision Framework

1. Keep essential obligations current and preserve adequate cash.
2. Rank each debt by its effective interest rate and whether that rate can change.
3. Identify employer matching and account-specific benefits.
4. Estimate investment returns as a range after fees and tax.
5. Compare the same amount over the same period and cash-flow timing.
6. Test what happens after a debt-rate increase and a major investment decline.
7. Consider a repeatable split if both certainty and asset growth have meaningful value.

## Common Mistakes

- Comparing a guaranteed debt rate with an optimistic gross investment return.
- Ignoring investment fees and tax.
- Treating all debt as though it has the same rate, terms, and risk.
- Investing while recurring expenses continue to return to credit.
- Using the emergency reserve for either option.
- Assuming debt consolidation solves the spending or cash-flow cause.

## FAQ

### What interest rate makes repayment mandatory?

There is no universal cutoff. Higher rates strengthen repayment, but employer matching, tax, liquidity, term, and household risk also matter.

### Is splitting the money irrational?

No. A split can balance certainty, liquidity, and long-term participation when neither option clearly dominates.

### Should mortgage debt use the same analysis?

The principles are similar, but mortgages add prepayment privileges, renewal risk, amortization, and housing concentration. Read [Pay Off the Mortgage or Invest?](/housing/pay-off-mortgage-or-invest).
