## Executive Summary

Debt repayment produces a certain saving equal to interest avoided, adjusted for tax treatment and any penalty. Investing offers an uncertain return, liquidity that varies by account, and the possibility of loss. Expensive consumer debt usually sets a hurdle that diversified investments cannot reliably clear. Low-rate debt creates a closer decision.

Before either option, protect essential payments and adequate liquidity. Then compare the same dollar over the same period after fees and tax. Include employer matching, registered-account value, debt-rate changes, and the cost of being wrong.

## Compare Rates on the Same Basis

For personal non-deductible debt:

> Debt repayment return ≈ interest rate avoided.

For a taxable investment:

> Net investment return = gross return minus fees minus applicable tax.

A $10,000 payment on 18% debt avoids about $1,800 of first-year interest if the balance would otherwise remain unchanged. A 7% investment earns $700 before fees and tax in the same simplified year, and can lose value. The debt case is materially stronger.

At a 4% loan rate, the saving is about $400. A long-term diversified investment may have a higher expected return, but not a guaranteed one. Liquidity and account tax treatment can then change the preference.

## The Case for Debt Repayment

Repayment is stronger when the rate is high or variable, cash flow is strained, the borrower values lower fixed obligations, or the investment horizon is short. It also reduces the amount exposed to rate increases and can improve resilience.

The benefit may be smaller when prepayment charges apply, interest is deductible in a valid income-earning context, or repayment consumes cash needed for emergencies. Tax deductibility is fact-specific and should not be assumed.

## The Case for Investing

Investing is stronger when the debt rate is low, the horizon is long, liquidity is adequate, the asset mix is appropriate, and valuable matching or registered-account opportunities are available. It also builds assets rather than only reducing liabilities.

Expected return is not a promise. The investor must be able to continue through a decline. Borrowing at 5% to hold an investment after a 30% loss can feel very different from comparing long-term averages on paper.

## Employer Match and Registered Accounts

An employer match can produce an immediate benefit that changes the sequence. Confirm vesting, plan costs, access, and eligible contribution limits.

A TFSA can protect investment growth and withdrawals from tax but contributions are not deductible. An RRSP may create a deduction but future withdrawals are taxable. The RRSP comparison should include the use of the tax reduction. Spending it weakens the long-term case.

## Three Scenarios

### Credit card at 20%

With a starter reserve and no employer match, repayment is usually the dominant financial case. The certain 20% interest saving is exceptionally difficult to match with a prudent investment.

### Line of credit at 8%

Repayment remains compelling, especially if the rate floats. A match may justify limited parallel investing. A split can preserve the habit of investing, but the debt cost should not be minimized.

### Fixed loan at 3%

With a long horizon, strong cash flow, and suitable registered room, investing can have a higher expected outcome. Repayment still offers certainty and lower obligations. Use a return range and test a major early decline.

## Opportunity Cost and Liquidity

Debt repayment can be difficult to reverse. A paid credit card creates available credit but not guaranteed access on the same terms. An investment account may be liquid, but market value can be lower when needed and RRSP withdrawals create tax and lost room.

Keep emergency and near-term goal money outside the comparison. A mathematically attractive choice can be poor if it forces later borrowing.

## Decision Framework

1. Keep obligations current and preserve adequate cash.
2. Rank debt by effective rate and variability.
3. Identify matching and account-specific benefits.
4. Estimate investment return as a range after fees and tax.
5. Compare the same period and cash-flow timing.
6. Test a rate increase and investment decline.
7. Consider a split if both certainty and asset growth have meaningful value.

## Common Mistakes

- Comparing a guaranteed debt rate with an optimistic gross return.
- Ignoring fees and tax.
- Treating all debt alike.
- Investing while recurring expenses return to credit.
- Using the emergency reserve for either option.
- Assuming a debt consolidation solves the spending or cash-flow cause.

## FAQ

### What interest rate makes repayment mandatory?

There is no universal cutoff. Higher rates strengthen repayment, but matching, tax, liquidity, term, and household risk matter.

### Is splitting the money irrational?

No. A split can balance certainty, liquidity, and long-term participation when neither option clearly dominates.

### Should mortgage debt use the same analysis?

The principles are similar, but mortgages add prepayment privileges, renewal risk, amortization, and housing concentration. Read [Pay Off the Mortgage or Invest?](/housing/pay-off-mortgage-or-invest).
