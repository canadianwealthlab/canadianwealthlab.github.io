## Executive Summary

Retirement readiness is the ability to fund spending from a combination of CPP or QPP, OAS, workplace pensions, employment income, and personal assets across an uncertain lifetime. Begin with spending, not an arbitrary portfolio multiple. Estimate reliable income by year, then calculate the gap personal assets must cover after tax.

The plan should model inflation, variable returns, longevity, account taxation, required RRIF withdrawals, and a response to weak early markets. CPP timing, OAS recovery tax, pension options, and mortgage decisions should be evaluated together rather than one at a time.

## Estimate Retirement Spending

Build spending from categories. Separate essential recurring costs, discretionary lifestyle costs, irregular capital expenses, and taxes. Remove work costs only when they will actually disappear, and add costs that may rise, such as travel early in retirement or home support later.

Use today's dollars first. If $70,000 after tax supports the intended first-year lifestyle, test lower and higher cases. A plan that works only at one precise spending level is fragile.

Inflate future nominal costs using:

> Future cost = current cost × (1 + inflation rate)^years.

At 2% inflation, $70,000 becomes about $85,300 after ten years. Personal inflation can differ from the Consumer Price Index because spending patterns vary.

## Map Reliable Income by Year

CPP depends on contribution history, earnings, and start age. OAS depends on age, residence history, and income, and may be reduced through the recovery tax. Defined-benefit pensions may have early-retirement reductions, bridge benefits, survivor options, and indexing provisions. Defined-contribution plans depend on contributions and investment results.

Use personal estimates from Service Canada and pension administrators. The 2026 maximum CPP retirement pension starting at age 65 is $1,507.65 per month, but the average for new age-65 beneficiaries reported for April 2026 was $877.01. The maximum is not a reasonable default for every plan.

Create a year-by-year income timeline because benefits and registered-account conversions begin at different ages.

## Calculate the Portfolio Gap

> Portfolio-funded spending = total spending and tax minus CPP, OAS, pensions, employment, and other reliable income.

If gross spending and tax total $85,000 and reliable income is $45,000, personal assets initially fund $40,000. Dividing $40,000 by a chosen withdrawal rate gives a rough portfolio reference, not a guarantee. At 4%, the reference is $1 million; at 3.5%, about $1.143 million.

Withdrawal-rate research depends on market data, asset allocation, fees, horizon, inflation, and a fixed spending rule. A real household can adapt spending, but may also face taxes and irregular costs excluded from simple research.

## Plan for Sequence Risk

Average return does not describe retirement safety. Poor returns early in retirement, combined with withdrawals, can permanently reduce the portfolio even if the long-run average later recovers.

Responses can include keeping near-term spending in stable assets, using a diversified allocation, lowering discretionary withdrawals after large declines, delaying major purchases, earning temporary income, or adjusting benefit timing. Each has a cost. Excess cash can reduce expected return, and spending cuts may be unrealistic.

## Coordinate Account Withdrawals

RRSP and RRIF withdrawals are generally taxable. TFSA withdrawals generally are not taxable and do not add to net income used for many benefit calculations. Taxable accounts can generate interest, dividends, foreign income, and capital gains.

A lowest-tax-this-year strategy can create larger RRIF balances and higher taxable income later. An intentional RRSP withdrawal before mandatory RRIF conversion can sometimes smooth lifetime tax, but the result depends on future rates, OAS recovery tax, credits, pension splitting eligibility, spouse or partner income, and estate considerations outside the scope of this platform. Use professional tax projections for consequential decisions.

## Decide When to Start CPP

Starting CPP before 65 generally reduces the monthly amount; delaying after 65 increases it up to age 70. The decision compares longevity protection and a larger inflation-adjusted lifetime payment with the value of receiving payments earlier and preserving other assets.

Health, expected longevity, employment, tax, survivor interactions, cash needs, and comfort with investment risk can change the answer. A simple break-even age ignores taxes, returns, and the insurance value of income that continues for life.

## Review Housing and Debt

Retiring with a mortgage is not automatically wrong. Compare the payment with reliable income, renewal risk, accessible reserves, and the tax and opportunity cost of using investments to repay it. Selling or downsizing should be treated as a scenario with transaction costs and timing uncertainty, not a guaranteed source of a specific amount.

Include property tax, maintenance, utilities, and accessibility needs whether or not the mortgage is paid off.

## A Five-Year Preparation Sequence

1. Build a detailed spending estimate and test a range.
2. Obtain personal CPP, OAS, and pension estimates.
3. Map taxable income and account withdrawals by year.
4. Review asset allocation against the withdrawal horizon.
5. Fund near-term irregular expenses separately.
6. Test weak early returns, higher inflation, and longer life.
7. Review debt and housing choices.
8. Update legal and account administration with qualified help where needed, without relying on this site for legal advice.

## Common Mistakes

- Using the maximum CPP amount instead of a personal estimate.
- Applying a withdrawal rule to gross assets without estimating tax.
- Assuming spending is constant in every year.
- Counting home equity without a realistic plan to access it.
- Holding an aggressive portfolio because the average expected return closes a shortfall.
- Converting all assets to cash and ignoring inflation and longevity.
- Evaluating CPP, OAS, RRIF, and pension choices separately.

## FAQ

### How many years should a retirement plan cover?

Use a horizon that reflects plausible longevity and household circumstances, then test longer. A plan for a couple must consider the period when one person may live much longer than the other.

### Is a 4% withdrawal rate safe?

No rate is universally safe. The historical result depends on market, horizon, allocation, fees, inflation, and spending rules. Use it only as one scenario.

### Should a mortgage be paid before retirement?

Not automatically. Compare payment capacity, rate and renewal risk, taxes on funds used for repayment, lost liquidity, and the household's preference for lower fixed obligations.
