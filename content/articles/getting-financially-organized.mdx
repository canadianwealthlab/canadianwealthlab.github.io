## Executive Summary

Financial organization means being able to answer a small set of questions quickly: what you own, what you owe, what cash must cover next, what deadlines are approaching, and which decision needs attention. It does not require tracking every purchase forever.

Build one inventory, one cash-flow view, one document system, and one review calendar. Keep enough detail to prevent missed obligations and support decisions. Avoid creating a system so elaborate that it becomes another abandoned project.

## Build a Financial Inventory

List chequing and savings accounts, registered and taxable investment accounts, credit cards, loans, mortgages, workplace plans, and major property. Record the institution, account purpose, current balance, interest rate where relevant, and how to access statements. Do not place passwords or full account numbers in an ordinary spreadsheet.

For debt, record the balance, rate, minimum payment, payment date, term, renewal date, and prepayment conditions. For investments, record the account type, goal, owner, and broad asset mix. Market values change, so note the date of the snapshot.

The inventory should expose forgotten accounts, duplicate fees, unused credit, promotional rates that expire, and assets that lack a defined purpose.

## Create a Cash-Flow View

Use reliable after-tax income, not a best month or annual gross salary. Separate spending into regular commitments, flexible costs, and irregular obligations. Convert annual or quarterly costs into a monthly provision.

Example: property tax of $4,800, annual professional dues of $900, vehicle maintenance of $1,200, and gifts of $1,200 create $8,100 of irregular annual costs. The monthly provision is $8,100 ÷ 12 = $675. If that provision is omitted, a supposed $700 monthly surplus is actually only $25.

Track the direction for two or three ordinary months. The purpose is to find the sustainable surplus, not to judge every spending category.

## Assign Accounts a Job

A simple structure can include:

- An operating account for income and routine bills.
- A reserve account for emergencies.
- One or more short-term goal accounts for predictable expenses.
- Registered and taxable investment accounts for longer goals.

Separate accounts are useful when they prevent accidental spending or make progress visible. They are unhelpful when they create fees, transfer delays, and reconciliation work without changing behaviour.

Automate fixed bills and planned transfers, but keep a buffer for timing differences. Review automatic transfers after income or major expenses change.

## Organize Documents and Deadlines

Keep tax slips, notices of assessment, contribution records, mortgage documents, loan agreements, pension statements, and major purchase records in a consistent system. Adjusted-cost-base records for taxable investments may be needed years later. Digital files should use clear dates and names, with secure backups and access controls appropriate to the sensitivity of the information.

Create calendar reminders for tax filing, instalments if applicable, mortgage renewal, promotional debt rates, benefit applications, account maturity dates, and the annual review. Start mortgage renewal preparation months before the maturity date rather than accepting the first offer under time pressure.

## Verify Registered Account Room

Do not infer room from account balances. TFSA room depends on eligibility years, contributions, prior-year withdrawals, and residence. RRSP deduction room depends on earned income, pension adjustments, unused room, and other factors shown on CRA records. FHSA room begins only after opening an account and is subject to annual, lifetime, and participation-period limits.

Reconcile CRA information with your own records because financial-institution reporting can lag. Keep direct transfers distinct from withdrawals and new contributions.

## Create a One-Page Decision Dashboard

The dashboard should show:

- Reliable monthly surplus after irregular provisions.
- Accessible emergency cash and months of essential spending covered.
- Debt balances, rates, and next renewal or promotional dates.
- Contributions made and verified remaining room.
- Progress toward the next two major goals.
- Decisions due within the next 12 months.

This is not a performance score. It is an exception report that directs attention to what changed.

## Common Mistakes

- Recording balances without the rate, purpose, or relevant deadline.
- Using an account aggregator as the only record when historical tax information must be preserved.
- Storing sensitive passwords beside the inventory.
- Treating irregular costs as unexpected emergencies.
- Assuming CRA account room is current to the day.
- Creating many labelled accounts without maintaining the transfers between them.
- Measuring progress only through investment market values.

## A Two-Hour Setup

1. Gather the latest statement for every account and debt.
2. Create the inventory and note missing information.
3. Estimate monthly cash flow with irregular provisions.
4. Label every account with a purpose.
5. List all deadlines over the next year.
6. Select one immediate correction, such as cancelling a fee, updating an automatic payment, or investigating expensive debt.
7. Schedule a 30-minute monthly check and an annual full review.

## FAQ

### Do I need financial software?

No. A spreadsheet or secure notes system can be enough. Use software only if automation and reporting save more time than setup and maintenance consume.

### How often should balances be updated?

Monthly is sufficient for cash flow and debt in many households. Long-term investments may need only quarterly or annual review unless a transaction or decision is pending.

### Should couples keep one combined inventory?

A shared household view can improve planning even when accounts remain separate. Each person should understand which obligations are joint and how information can be accessed if one person is unavailable.
