## Executive Summary

The FHSA can be compelling for an eligible first-time buyer because contributions are generally deductible and qualifying home withdrawals are tax-free. A TFSA provides flexible tax-free growth and withdrawals but no contribution deduction. An RRSP can reduce current taxable income and defer tax, while withdrawals are generally taxable and room is not restored.

The answer depends on the goal, eligibility, room, current and future tax rates, access, employer matching, benefit interactions, and whether an RRSP tax reduction is saved. Account choice does not determine the investment. Near-term home money may still need stable assets.

## Core Mechanics for 2026

The 2026 TFSA annual dollar limit is $7,000. Personal room also reflects eligible prior years, unused room, and prior-year withdrawals. Withdrawals generally create equivalent room on January 1 of the next year.

FHSA participation room is generally $8,000 annually with a $40,000 lifetime limit, subject to carry-forward and participation-period rules. Room begins only after an account is opened. Qualifying withdrawals require the current conditions to be met.

RRSP deduction room is generally based on 18% of prior-year earned income up to the annual dollar limit, adjusted for pension participation and other items, plus unused room. The 2026 dollar maximum is $33,810. Use the deduction limit on personal CRA records, not the annual maximum.

## Tax Treatment

TFSA contributions use after-tax dollars. Qualifying growth and withdrawals are generally tax-free.

RRSP and FHSA contributions may be deductible, subject to limits. RRSP withdrawals are generally taxable. A qualifying FHSA withdrawal for a home is generally non-taxable. A non-qualifying FHSA withdrawal is generally taxable, while eligible transfers to an RRSP or RRIF may be possible without using RRSP room.

## The Case for an FHSA

An FHSA is stronger when the holder is eligible, a qualifying first-home purchase is a real goal, and the account can be used within the participation period. The combination of a deduction and non-taxable qualifying withdrawal can dominate the same contribution to a TFSA or RRSP under those conditions.

It is weaker when eligibility is uncertain, the home goal is not credible, or access may be needed for another purpose. Confirm the first-time home buyer definition, residency, account-opening age, qualifying home, written agreement, occupancy intention, and timing rules.

## FHSA Versus the Home Buyers' Plan

The Home Buyers' Plan permits eligible RRSP withdrawals, currently up to $60,000, for a qualifying home. HBP amounts generally must be repaid over time or included in income according to the rules. FHSA qualifying withdrawals do not require repayment. Eligible buyers may use both.

The HBP can make existing RRSP savings available, but withdrawing removes invested assets and creates future repayment obligations. An RRSP contribution made shortly before an HBP withdrawal can be subject to deduction limitations, so verify timing.

## The Case for a TFSA

A TFSA is stronger for flexible goals, lower current tax rates, or withdrawals that should not add to taxable income. Restored room improves long-run flexibility, but not until the next calendar year.

A withdrawal and same-year replacement can create an excess contribution if no other room exists. Direct transfers between TFSAs should be completed through issuers rather than by withdrawing and recontributing.

## The Case for an RRSP

An RRSP is stronger when current marginal tax is high relative to expected withdrawal tax, retirement is the goal, and the tax reduction is saved or used productively. Employer matching can make an RRSP or workplace plan an earlier priority.

The refund is not free money. A $10,000 deduction at 40% may reduce current tax by about $4,000, but the full future RRSP withdrawal is generally taxable. Comparing a $10,000 RRSP deposit with a $10,000 TFSA deposit ignores the different pre-tax cost unless the tax reduction is included.

## Scenarios

An eligible first-time buyer at a 35% marginal rate may prioritize an FHSA for a five-year purchase, while holding stable investments. A lower-income worker saving for several possible goals may favour TFSA flexibility. A high-income worker with a long retirement horizon and lower expected retirement rate may prioritize an employer match and RRSP, then use a TFSA.

These are examples, not prescriptions. Benefits, pension income, future rates, and the actual use of withdrawals can reverse the preference.

## Decision Framework

1. Define the goal and withdrawal date.
2. Verify personal eligibility and room.
3. Capture employer matching where appropriate.
4. Compare current deduction value with future withdrawal treatment.
5. Account for benefit interactions and flexibility.
6. Choose investments separately based on horizon and risk.
7. Revisit as income and goals change.

## Common Mistakes

- Using annual limits as personal contribution room.
- Ignoring the FHSA account-opening requirement.
- Investing near-term home money too aggressively.
- Spending the RRSP tax reduction while comparing outcomes as if invested.
- Recontributing TFSA withdrawals in the same year without room.
- Assuming the HBP is tax-free money with no repayment obligation.

## FAQ

### Can all three accounts be used?

Yes, when eligible and when each serves a purpose. Contribution capacity, cash flow, and administrative complexity can limit the benefit.

### Is FHSA always first for an eligible buyer?

Not always. Expensive debt, liquidity, employer matching, purchase uncertainty, and account timing can change the order.

### Should the highest-return investment go in the TFSA?

Tax-free growth can make that attractive, but risk, horizon, foreign withholding, and the cost of losses must also be considered.
