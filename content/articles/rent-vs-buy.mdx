## Quick Answer

Buying tends to become more attractive with a long stay, manageable total ownership costs, and a home price that fits without sacrificing other goals. Renting can be stronger when flexibility matters, the price-to-rent gap is wide, or the renter consistently invests the savings. Compare net worth and lifestyle resilience, not rent versus the mortgage payment alone.

## The Decision

A home is both shelter and a leveraged asset. Buying converts cash into down payment, closing costs, equity, maintenance, and transaction exposure. Renting purchases flexibility and transfers many property risks to a landlord, but does not create home equity.

The result depends heavily on uncertain variables: future prices, interest rates, investment returns, rent growth, maintenance, and how long you stay.

## Key Factors

### Time horizon

Buying and selling have meaningful transaction costs. A longer stay gives ownership more time to absorb those costs. If work, family, or location could change soon, flexibility has measurable value.

### Total carrying cost

Include mortgage interest, property tax, insurance, maintenance, condo fees, and opportunity cost. Principal repayment builds equity and should not be treated as a pure expense.

### The alternative investment

The renter comparison is only credible if the down payment and any ongoing monthly savings are actually invested. Assuming perfect investing discipline can overstate the renter outcome.

## Scenario Analysis

### Expensive market, modest rent

When comparable rent is low relative to the purchase price, renting can preserve capital and reduce concentration in one property and city.

### Long stay and stable finances

Buying may fit when the household expects to stay for many years, can absorb repairs and rate changes, and values control of the home.

### Thin monthly margin

If ownership would consume nearly all available cash flow, the financial and lifestyle risk may outweigh an optimistic appreciation forecast.

## Common Mistakes

- Comparing rent only with the mortgage payment.
- Treating principal repayment as an expense or ignoring it entirely.
- Assuming home prices or stocks will repeat one historical period.
- Forgetting selling costs and major maintenance.
- Buying because of social pressure rather than a durable plan.

## Decision Framework

Compare a genuinely similar home. Estimate the full upfront and monthly ownership cost, model five- and ten-year outcomes, and test conservative assumptions. Then evaluate non-financial priorities: stability, control, mobility, and maintenance responsibility.

## FAQ

### Is renting throwing money away?

No. Rent purchases housing and flexibility. Mortgage interest, property tax, insurance, maintenance, and transaction costs also do not build equity.

### How long should I stay before buying makes sense?

There is no universal break-even period. It depends on transaction costs, price-to-rent ratio, financing, appreciation, and investment returns.

### Should I count home appreciation?

Yes, but use a range of assumptions. A purchase decision that only works with aggressive appreciation may have little margin for error.

## Build the Economic Comparison

Compare unrecoverable housing costs, equity accumulation, and invested cash over the same holding period. For renting, include rent, tenant insurance, utilities, moving costs, and the return on the down payment and monthly savings that remain invested. For owning, include mortgage interest, property tax, maintenance, insurance, fees, transaction costs, and opportunity cost. Mortgage principal is a cash outflow that builds equity, so do not count it as both a cost and an asset.

> Owner ending wealth = home value minus selling costs minus mortgage balance plus remaining financial assets.

> Renter ending wealth = invested initial cash plus invested monthly difference minus moving costs.

Use after-tax investment returns where applicable. A principal residence may qualify for the principal-residence exemption, subject to rules and reporting. Rent does not create a taxable asset, but invested savings can generate tax depending on the account.

## Transaction Costs and Holding Period

Buying can require transfer tax, legal services, inspection, appraisal, mortgage insurance, moving, and immediate repairs. Selling can require brokerage commission, legal work, mortgage discharge, penalties, moving, and preparation. A short stay gives these costs little time to be recovered.

Model three, seven, and fifteen years rather than assuming the household stays indefinitely. A move can be triggered by work, family, space, relationship, or health rather than market conditions.

## Worked Cases

Assume rent is $2,800 monthly and an alternative home costs $700,000. The buyer has $140,000 for a 20% down payment plus separate closing cash. Build owner cost from interest, tax, insurance, maintenance, fees, and utilities, then compare it with rent. Invest the down payment and any renter monthly saving at a conservative after-fee return range.

In a short-hold case, assume no home appreciation and include purchase and sale costs. Renting can remain stronger even when the mortgage payment resembles rent. In a long-hold case with moderate appreciation and disciplined renter investing, either option can lead depending on rates, maintenance, and returns. In a stress case, combine a home-price decline, higher renewal rate, and major repair.

## Non-Financial Value and Local Rules

Ownership can provide control, stability, and protection from a landlord ending the tenancy, subject to mortgage and property obligations. Renting can provide mobility, fewer repair responsibilities, and lower transaction friction. These are legitimate benefits and should be named.

Tenancy law, rent controls, transfer taxes, buyer programs, strata rules, and property tax vary. Local supply and price-to-rent relationships can make a national average irrelevant. Use current provincial and municipal sources.

## Complete Decision Checklist

1. Define a realistic holding-period range.
2. Set a household affordability limit, not the lender maximum.
3. Calculate all purchase, ownership, and sale costs.
4. Separate mortgage principal from economic cost.
5. Model renter investments consistently.
6. Stress rates, repairs, prices, and a move.
7. Record the non-financial benefits that matter.
