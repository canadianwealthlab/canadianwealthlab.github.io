## Executive Summary

The true cost of a major purchase includes acquisition, financing, tax, operation, maintenance, transaction, and opportunity cost. Irregular income should be allocated only after tax and near-term obligations are reserved. A one-time inflow should not automatically support a permanent monthly commitment.

Use a cooling-off period for reversible timing, calculate the all-in cost, and compare the purchase with realistic alternatives. For a windfall, restore the financial foundation before optimizing accounts or investments.

## Calculate the Full Purchase Cost

> Full cost = purchase price + tax + financing + recurring operation + maintenance + transaction cost minus realistic resale value.

A $50,000 vehicle financed over several years can also require sales tax, interest, insurance, fuel or charging, parking, maintenance, and eventual selling costs. Comparing only the monthly payment hides term and total interest.

For a renovation, include design, permits, temporary accommodation, financing, contingency, and whether the change creates ongoing maintenance. For a property, include transaction costs and concentration risk.

## Add Opportunity Cost

Cash used for the purchase cannot reduce debt, remain liquid, or be invested. Estimate the alternative value using a conservative range.

If $30,000 invested for ten years earned a hypothetical 3% to 7% after fees, it could grow to roughly $40,300 to $59,000 before applicable tax. That is not a forecast or a reason to avoid the purchase. It makes the tradeoff visible.

## Test Affordability, Not Approval

Financing approval does not account for every goal. Add the payment to existing fixed commitments and test lower income, higher rates if variable, and the complete ownership cost.

Avoid extending a loan only to make the monthly payment look manageable. A longer term may increase total interest and keep the obligation after the asset has materially depreciated.

## Allocate a Bonus or Windfall

Start with the amount actually available after tax. Payroll withholding may differ from final tax, and self-employed revenue may require income tax, CPP, and GST or HST reserves.

A practical sequence is:

1. Reserve tax and known obligations.
2. Restore operating cash and emergency reserves.
3. Repay expensive debt.
4. Fund near-term goals.
5. Evaluate FHSA, TFSA, RRSP, mortgage, and taxable investing.
6. Allocate intentional spending without creating an unaffordable recurring cost.

The sequence changes with matching, account room, debt rates, and time horizon.

## Variable Income

Base fixed commitments on conservative reliable income. Hold a larger cash range when commissions or contracts are volatile. Use a quarterly allocation rule for genuine surplus after taxes and business needs.

Example: a $40,000 gross bonus is not a $40,000 investment decision. First estimate final tax, correct underwithholding, upcoming annual costs, and any income gap the bonus must support. Only the remainder is unassigned.

## Compare Three Cases

### Purchase now with financing

Preserves some cash but adds interest and fixed payments. Stronger when the purchase is urgent and financing is affordable under stress.

### Save and purchase later

Avoids or reduces interest and creates time to confirm the need. Weaker when delay has a material practical cost or prices change unfavourably.

### Buy a lower-cost alternative

Can preserve the function while reducing purchase and opportunity cost. It is not automatically best if reliability, operating cost, or replacement frequency is worse.

## Compare Financing in Dollars

Obtain the annual percentage rate, term, payment frequency, fees, optional products, total payments, and prepayment terms. Compare the cash price with the total financed cost. A low payment can result from a longer term, large final payment, or down payment rather than a lower cost.

If the rate is promotional, confirm which discount or rebate is lost by financing. For variable borrowing, test the payment or amortization at a higher rate. Do not use the emergency fund as the down payment unless the remaining reserve still fits the household risk.

## Create a Windfall Decision Record

Write the gross amount, estimated tax, amount available, allocations, and reasons. For a material inheritance or legal settlement, pause before making irreversible gifts, investments, property purchases, or permanent spending commitments. Temporary insured cash can create decision space while ownership, tax, and estate questions are confirmed.

A written allocation also prevents the same dollars from being mentally assigned to debt repayment, investing, and spending at once.

## Common Mistakes

- Comparing monthly payments rather than full cost.
- Ignoring sales tax, maintenance, and disposal.
- Treating gross irregular income as spendable.
- Increasing housing, vehicle, or subscription commitments after one strong year.
- Investing money owed for tax.
- Assuming every purchase must be justified by financial return.

## Decision Checklist

1. Define the purpose and acceptable alternatives.
2. Calculate full cost and opportunity cost.
3. Test payment under lower income and higher expenses.
4. Separate tax and required reserves from a windfall.
5. Avoid permanent commitments based on temporary cash.
6. Choose a deliberate amount for enjoyment when affordable.
7. Record the assumptions that would change the decision.

## FAQ

### Is spending part of a windfall irresponsible?

Not inherently. The question is whether obligations and priority risks are addressed and the spending is intentional rather than creating an unsustainable commitment.

### How long should I wait before a major purchase?

There is no fixed period. Use enough time to verify the need, full cost, alternatives, and affordability. Urgent repairs require a different process from discretionary purchases.

### Should a bonus always go to an RRSP?

No. Compare tax rate, room, employer plans, liquidity, debt, goals, and future withdrawals. The source of cash does not determine the best account.
