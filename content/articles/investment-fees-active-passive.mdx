## Executive Summary

Fees reduce investor returns with certainty; gross active outperformance is uncertain. Passive strategies seek market or index exposure at low cost. Active strategies seek to outperform or manage risk through security selection, allocation, or other choices. Some will outperform, but identifying them in advance and sustaining the advantage after fees and tax is difficult.

Compare the complete service, not one fund fee. Advice can include planning, tax coordination, portfolio management, and behavioural support. The relevant question is whether those benefits justify the all-in cost for this household.

## Inventory Every Cost

- Fund management expense ratio and trading expenses.
- Advisory or portfolio-management fee.
- Commissions, spreads, and currency conversion.
- Account administration and transfer fees.
- Tax drag from turnover, distributions, and account placement.
- Cash drag and implementation delay.

Ask which fees are deducted from the account, billed separately, embedded in a fund, or paid by a third party. Compensation can create conflicts even when the investor does not see an invoice.

## Compounding Example

Invest $200,000 for 25 years with no additions. At 6% after all costs, the future value is about $858,000. At 5%, it is about $677,000, a difference of roughly $181,000. Actual returns will not be smooth, but the arithmetic shows that one recurring percentage point is material.

Lower cost is valuable only when the portfolios and services are otherwise comparable.

## The Passive Case

Broad index strategies offer transparent exposure, low turnover, low fees, and reduced reliance on a manager. They accept market returns before costs and do not protect against market-wide declines. Index construction can create concentration and must still be understood.

## The Active Case

Active management can provide differentiated exposure, tax management, risk controls, or access to a deliberate process. Success depends on skill, capacity, cost, discipline, and investor patience. Underperformance can persist for years even for a sound process.

The strongest case is a clearly defined role and evidence that the expected benefit is not consumed by fees and tax. The weakest case relies on recent outperformance or a confident narrative.

## Advice Versus Product

Separate portfolio cost from planning value. An advisor may provide retirement projections, tax coordination, behaviour coaching, and administration beyond fund selection. Ask for the dollar cost at the current balance, services delivered, credentials, conflicts, and exit process.

Self-directed investing lowers some fees but transfers responsibility and error risk to the investor. It is not free when time, trading mistakes, or abandonment are included.

## Decision Framework

1. Define the exposure and service required.
2. Calculate all-in percentage and dollar costs.
3. Compare after-fee, risk-adjusted, and tax-aware outcomes over appropriate periods.
4. Assess whether active differences are persistent and understandable.
5. Value planning services separately.
6. Test whether the strategy can be maintained through underperformance.

## Common Mistakes

- Comparing active returns before fees with index returns after fees.
- Selecting a manager from a short winning period.
- Ignoring advice fees outside fund MERs.
- Assuming index investing requires no allocation decisions.
- Moving taxable assets without calculating gains.
- Paying for services that are not used or documented.

## FAQ

### Can active funds outperform?

Yes, some do over particular periods. The challenge is identifying future winners and retaining the advantage after cost, tax, and behaviour.

### Is an advisor fee always too high?

No. Evaluate services, quality, conflicts, and dollar cost. A fee can be worthwhile or poor value depending on what is delivered.

### Does passive mean no risk?

No. A passive fund carries the full market, concentration, currency, and product risks of its tracked exposure.

## Compare Advice Proposals in Dollars

Assume two portfolios each hold $500,000. Proposal A costs 0.25% for funds plus 1% for advice, or about $6,250 in the first year before tax and trading. Proposal B costs 0.20% for funds with no ongoing advice, or about $1,000. The $5,250 difference is not automatically waste or value.

List what A provides: a current financial plan, retirement and tax projections, portfolio implementation, rebalancing, behaviour support, meeting frequency, and coordination. Estimate what those services would cost separately and whether the household will use them. For B, include the investor's time, trading, planning software, tax help, and the risk that the portfolio is abandoned.

## Performance Reporting

Ask for time-weighted and money-weighted returns, an appropriate benchmark, complete fees, and periods that include weak markets. Time-weighted return helps evaluate management independent of deposits; money-weighted return reflects the investor's actual cash-flow experience.

Compare the portfolio's risk and asset mix with the benchmark. A lower-equity portfolio should not be condemned for trailing equities in a rising market without considering its intended risk.

## Exit and Conflict Review

Understand transfer fees, deferred charges if any, embedded taxable gains, proprietary products, referral compensation, and whether the advisor is limited to certain products. Ask how the relationship ends and how records are transferred. Complexity can make a fee relationship difficult to leave even when the stated contract is simple.
