## Executive Summary

Estimate retirement spending from categories, then subtract CPP, OAS, pensions, and other reliable income. The remaining after-tax gap is what personal assets must fund. A withdrawal rate is the first-year portfolio withdrawal divided by initial assets. It is a scenario tool, not a universal safe rate.

Historical withdrawal research depends on market, allocation, horizon, inflation rule, fees, tax, and spending flexibility. Canadian households should model account taxation and public benefits rather than applying a US research number directly.

## Estimate Spending

Separate essential recurring spending, discretionary spending, irregular capital costs, and taxes. Use today's dollars. Remove employment costs only where they disappear and add travel, home maintenance, health, or support costs where appropriate.

Test a base, lower, and higher case. Spending often changes through retirement and is not perfectly indexed.

## Calculate the Gap

> Portfolio-funded amount = spending plus tax minus reliable income.

If spending and tax total $90,000 and CPP, OAS, and pension income total $50,000, the initial gap is $40,000. On a $1 million portfolio, the initial withdrawal rate is 4%. On $1.2 million it is 3.33%.

The portfolio balance may include taxable RRSP or RRIF assets, tax-free TFSA assets, and taxable investments. Equal balances do not provide equal after-tax spending.

## Understand Research Limits

Classic withdrawal studies often assume a fixed inflation-adjusted annual withdrawal and a specific stock-bond portfolio over a historical period. Real households have taxes, fees, benefit changes, irregular costs, and different markets. They may adapt spending, but essential costs limit flexibility.

A rate that survived every tested historical period is not guaranteed to survive future returns, inflation, longevity, or policy changes.

## Longevity and Inflation

Longer horizons increase the risk of depletion and cumulative inflation. A couple's plan should cover the possibility that one partner lives well beyond average. OAS and CPP have inflation adjustments under program rules, while workplace pension indexing varies.

Use the Bank of Canada target midpoint only as one scenario. Test higher inflation, especially for household-specific costs.

## Flexible Spending

Rules can reduce discretionary spending after poor returns, use percentage-of-portfolio withdrawals, or set floors and ceilings. Flexibility can improve portfolio durability but creates variable lifestyle income.

Define which costs can change and by how much. A model that cuts essential spending by 25% may not be realistic.

## Common Mistakes

- Applying a rate to gross assets without tax.
- Using maximum CPP instead of a personal estimate.
- Ignoring irregular home and vehicle costs.
- Assuming spending rises exactly with CPI.
- Treating historical survival as a guarantee.
- Counting home equity with no access plan.
- Choosing a higher rate because returns were recently strong.

## Planning Checklist

1. Build spending by category and scenario.
2. Obtain personal benefit and pension estimates.
3. Map account taxation by year.
4. Calculate the portfolio gap.
5. Test several starting rates and return sequences.
6. Define realistic spending flexibility.
7. Review annually.

## FAQ

### Is 4% safe in Canada?

No percentage is universally safe. Use it as a reference scenario and model Canadian tax, benefits, fees, allocation, horizon, and flexibility.

### Should spending exclude mortgage principal?

No for cash-flow planning. The payment must be funded. For economic analysis, separate principal from interest because principal builds equity.

### Does spending always fall with age?

Not for every household. Some discretionary categories may fall while home support or health-related costs rise. Use scenarios.

## Sequence-of-Returns Risk

Average return does not describe withdrawal safety. Poor returns early in retirement, combined with asset sales, can permanently reduce the base available for recovery. Two portfolios with the same returns in opposite order can finish at very different values.

Assume $1 million and a $40,000 initial withdrawal indexed with inflation. A 25% decline in year one requires more units to fund spending and leaves fewer assets for a later recovery. The same decline near the end of retirement has less time to compound through withdrawals.

Sequence risk grows with the withdrawal rate, equity volatility, inflexible spending, and dependence on the portfolio before reliable benefits begin.

## Stable Near-Term Assets

Cash, GICs, and high-quality short bonds can fund planned withdrawals without an immediate equity sale. The amount should be tied to the portfolio-funded spending gap, not a generic number of years.

Too much cash lowers expected return and creates inflation and reinvestment risk. Define when stable assets are refilled. A bucket without a rule can become market timing.

## Flexible Withdrawal Methods

- Skip an inflation increase after a poor year.
- Reduce a defined discretionary amount when the funded ratio falls.
- Withdraw a percentage of current assets, creating variable income.
- Use a spending floor and ceiling.
- Delay large discretionary purchases.

If essential spending is $55,000 and discretionary spending $20,000, a rule may allow discretionary cuts up to $10,000 after a severe decline. It should not assume essential bills can be cut by the same percentage.

## Coordinate Benefits and Withdrawals

Delaying CPP can increase lifetime protected income but requires assets before it starts. That can increase early portfolio withdrawals while reducing late-life longevity risk. Map both effects.

RRSP or RRIF withdrawals are taxable; TFSA withdrawals generally are not. A withdrawal rule should use after-tax cash and consider OAS recovery, pension start dates, and required RRIF minimums.

## Retirement Stress Test

1. Apply a major equity decline in the first two years.
2. Increase inflation for several years.
3. Add an irregular $30,000 cost.
4. Delay or reduce one expected income source.
5. Extend the planning horizon by five years.
6. Test the spending adjustment the household would actually make.

The response can combine stable assets, rebalancing, lower discretionary spending, temporary work, delayed purchases, or benefit timing. No single tool eliminates risk.

## Sequence Mistakes

- Using a smooth average return.
- Holding cash without a refill rule.
- Calling spending flexible when most costs are essential.
- Increasing equity to repair a shortfall near retirement.
- Starting CPP solely to reduce current withdrawals without comparing lifetime income.
- Assuming bonds cannot lose value.
