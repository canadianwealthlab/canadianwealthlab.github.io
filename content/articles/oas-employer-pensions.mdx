## Executive Summary

OAS is funded from general government revenue and is based primarily on age and residence history, not employment contributions. It is taxable and can be reduced through the recovery tax at higher individual net income. Payment amounts and thresholds change, so use current government figures.

A defined-benefit pension promises a formula-based payment subject to plan terms. A defined-contribution plan provides an account whose retirement income depends on contributions, investments, fees, and withdrawal choices. Read the actual plan, especially early-retirement reductions, bridge benefits, indexing, survivor options, and portability.

## OAS Mechanics

OAS generally begins at 65 when eligibility is met and can be delayed to 70 for a higher payment. Residence in Canada after age 18 affects full or partial pension eligibility, with international social-security agreements potentially relevant.

OAS is taxable. The recovery tax uses individual net income and annual thresholds. Household income allocation and withdrawals can affect the result, but aggressive income shifting is outside this guide.

## Defined-Benefit Pensions

The formula often uses pensionable earnings, service, and an accrual rate. Review normal retirement age, early reduction, unreduced eligibility, bridge benefits that end near 65, indexing, survivor pension, guarantee period, and integration with CPP or QPP.

A bridge ending can reduce pension income just as CPP begins. Do not count both permanently.

## Defined-Contribution Plans

The retirement value is not guaranteed. Review employer matching, vesting, fees, investment choices, locked-in rules, and transfer options. At retirement, funds may move to a locked-in retirement account, life income fund, annuity, or other permitted vehicle depending on jurisdiction and plan.

## Pension Options

Commuted-value transfers and pension elections can be irreversible, tax-sensitive, and jurisdiction-specific. Compare guaranteed lifetime income with investment control, inflation protection, survivor needs, transfer limits, and plan or sponsor risk. This platform does not recommend a universal election.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating OAS as contribution-based.
- Using a current maximum as a personal estimate.
- Ignoring OAS recovery tax.
- Counting a bridge pension for life.
- Assuming every pension is indexed.
- Comparing a lump sum with lifetime income without longevity and investment risk.
- Missing locked-in jurisdiction rules.

## Checklist

1. Obtain current OAS eligibility and payment information.
2. Get the complete pension booklet and personal estimate.
3. Identify bridge, indexing, survivor, and early-retirement terms.
4. Map income by age.
5. Model tax and OAS recovery.
6. Obtain regulated advice before an irreversible pension election.

## FAQ

### Is OAS guaranteed for every Canadian at 65?

Eligibility depends on legal status and residence history, and payment can be partial or recovered based on income. Verify the current rules.

### Is a defined-benefit pension risk-free?

It reduces individual market and longevity risk but remains subject to plan terms, inflation treatment, sponsor and funding framework, and legislative protections.

### Should OAS always be delayed?

No. Longevity, income, tax, cash needs, and other assets change the comparison.

## OAS Delay and Recovery Examples

Delaying OAS after 65 increases the monthly pension under current rules up to age 70, but gives up payments in the interim. Compare after-tax cumulative income, longevity, and what funds spending while waiting. Do not combine the delay increase with a maximum payment the person has not earned through residence.

The recovery tax uses individual net income and annual thresholds. A large RRIF withdrawal, capital gain, employment income, or pension can increase recovery. Avoiding every dollar is not necessarily the goal if a withdrawal improves lifetime tax or reduces risk.

## Defined-Benefit Example

Suppose a pension pays $42,000 from age 60 to 65, including a $10,000 bridge, then $32,000 after 65. Counting $42,000 for life overstates later income by $10,000. CPP and OAS may begin around the same time, but their tax and amounts differ.

Review whether the pension is indexed fully, partially, conditionally, or not at all. A non-indexed amount loses purchasing power over a long retirement.

## Defined-Contribution Example

Assume a $600,000 plan balance at retirement. It is an asset, not promised income. The sustainable withdrawal depends on allocation, fees, tax, horizon, and sequence. Locked-in jurisdiction rules can limit withdrawals and determine account types.

## Pension Election Framework

1. Obtain plan-specific written estimates.
2. Identify indexing, bridge, reduction, and survivor terms.
3. Model both partners and a survivor case where relevant.
4. Compare lifetime after-tax income and liquidity.
5. Include investment, inflation, and longevity risk.
6. Review transfer limits and locked-in rules.
7. Obtain regulated advice before an irreversible election.

## Annual Review Items

Update OAS amounts and recovery thresholds, pension indexing, tax withholding, beneficiary information, and the income timeline each year. Check whether a bridge payment ends, a pension election deadline approaches, or a change in residence affects eligibility. Use personal estimates from the plan and government rather than substituting a published maximum.
