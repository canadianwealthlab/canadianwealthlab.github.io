## Quick Answer

A useful retirement plan starts with expected spending, subtracts reliable income from CPP, OAS, and workplace pensions, and funds the remaining gap from savings. Model a range rather than one perfect forecast, then revisit it when markets, inflation, health, family, housing, or work plans change.

## Define the Retirement You Are Funding

Estimate spending in today’s dollars. Separate essential costs from flexible costs and one-time goals. Housing, travel, support for family, health and care, vehicles, and taxes can change materially after work ends.

Do not simply replace a fixed percentage of salary. Two households with the same employment income can need very different retirement income because their spending, debt, pensions, and goals differ.

## Inventory Every Income Source

List expected CPP or QPP, OAS, defined-benefit pension income, annuities, employment income, rental income, and withdrawals from registered and non-registered savings.

Use your own CPP estimate rather than the published maximum. CPP depends on contribution history, earnings, exclusions, and start age. OAS depends primarily on residence history and can be reduced at higher income.

## Calculate the Funding Gap

Subtract reliable after-tax income from estimated after-tax spending. The remaining annual gap must be supported by investments, savings, work, or lower spending.

A simple rule of thumb can provide a starting range, but it is not a plan. Sustainable withdrawals depend on the retirement length, asset mix, fees, taxes, inflation, market sequence, and willingness to adjust.

Use the [FIRE calculator](/calculators/fire) to test assumptions, then compare its result with the Government of Canada’s retirement-income calculator.

## Plan for Inflation and Longevity

Inflation reduces purchasing power even when the number of dollars stays the same. Some pension income is indexed, some is partially indexed, and some is not. Confirm the rules for each source.

Planning only to average life expectancy creates a real risk of outliving assets. Include a longer-life case and consider how spending might change later, including care and support needs.

## Coordinate Accounts and Taxes

RRSP and RRIF withdrawals are generally taxable. TFSA withdrawals are generally tax-free. Non-registered investments can produce interest, dividends, and capital gains with different tax treatment.

The withdrawal sequence can affect lifetime tax, income-tested benefits, estate value, and flexibility. A low-tax year before CPP or a pension begins may create a useful opportunity for planned registered-account withdrawals, but this requires household-specific analysis.

## Prepare for Market Risk

Poor returns early in retirement can be especially damaging when withdrawals continue. Hold an asset mix that reflects both the need for growth and the capacity to absorb losses.

Possible safeguards include a cash reserve for near-term spending, diversified investments, flexible discretionary spending, gradual retirement, and a written rule for responding to weak markets. None removes risk, but each can reduce forced decisions.

## Annual Retirement Review

- Update expected spending and major one-time costs.
- Retrieve current CPP, pension, and account estimates.
- Recalculate the income gap in today’s dollars.
- Review asset mix, fees, beneficiaries, and insurance.
- Test higher inflation, lower returns, and longer life.
- Coordinate CPP, OAS, pension, RRSP or RRIF, and TFSA decisions.
- Confirm wills, powers of attorney, and estate instructions separately with qualified legal advice.

## FAQ

### How much do I need to retire in Canada?

There is no reliable national number. The required portfolio depends on spending, housing, pensions, CPP and OAS, taxes, retirement date, longevity, investment risk, and flexibility.

### Does CPP replace the need to save?

Usually not by itself. CPP is one layer of retirement income. Its role depends on your contribution history and household spending.

### How often should I update my plan?

Review it at least annually and after major changes such as a job transition, move, inheritance, illness, market decline, or change in retirement date.
