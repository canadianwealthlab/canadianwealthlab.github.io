## Executive Summary

An investment plan begins with the liability: how much money is needed, when, and how flexible the goal is. Short-term required money belongs in stable, accessible assets. Long-term money can accept more market risk when the household has liquidity, a sustainable contribution plan, and the ability to remain invested.

Asset allocation determines the mix of equities, fixed income, and cash. Diversification reduces dependence on one company, sector, country, currency, or economic outcome. A written investment policy turns those choices into rules for contributions, ranges, rebalancing, withdrawals, and valid changes. Products are selected only after the policy exists.

## Saving Versus Investing

Saving emphasizes availability and nominal principal. Eligible deposits and short-term instruments can fit emergencies and known purchases but may lose purchasing power after inflation and tax. Investing accepts uncertain outcomes to pursue growth, income, or inflation resilience.

Define the goal first. A $60,000 down payment needed in two years has little capacity for loss if a decline would cancel the purchase. Retirement contributions invested for 25 years have a longer recovery period, but the investor still needs to tolerate severe declines without selling.

Do not invest required tax, operating, emergency, or closing cash merely because long-run expected returns are higher.

## Four Dimensions of Risk

- **Risk capacity:** the financial ability to absorb loss without changing an essential goal.
- **Risk tolerance:** the willingness to experience uncertainty and remain with the plan.
- **Return need:** the return required for contributions and timing to meet the goal.
- **Time horizon:** the period until withdrawals and how long withdrawals continue.

A high return need can mean the plan is underfunded. Increasing equity does not fix the arithmetic with certainty. Contributions, timing, spending, and goal flexibility must also be tested.

## Choose Asset Allocation

Equities represent ownership and offer higher expected long-run growth, with substantial volatility and possible permanent loss in individual companies. High-quality bonds can reduce portfolio variability and provide income, but carry interest-rate, credit, and inflation risk. Cash supports immediate obligations but usually has a lower expected real return.

An 80% equity portfolio should be expected to fall more than a 40% equity portfolio during a severe market decline. Historical drawdowns illustrate risk but cannot define the maximum future loss.

Use a range. If $10,000 is contributed at each year-end for 20 years, hypothetical returns of 3%, 5%, and 7% produce roughly $269,000, $331,000, and $410,000. The spread is assumption sensitivity, not evidence that selecting more risk will deliver the high case.

## Convert Nominal to Real Return

Inflation reduces purchasing power.

> Real return = (1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation) - 1.

A 5% nominal return with 2% inflation is about 2.94% real. Taxes and fees reduce the investor's result further. Use today's dollars with real returns or future dollars with nominal returns consistently.

## Diversify the Whole Household

Investment accounts are only part of exposure. Employment, pension, home, rental property, and employer stock can concentrate a household in one country or industry. A Canadian worker with Canadian real estate and bank shares has more domestic risk than the portfolio statement alone shows.

Diversify equities across companies, sectors, market sizes, and countries. Diversify fixed income by issuer quality and term where appropriate. Global diversification introduces currency and withholding-tax considerations but reduces reliance on one market.

Several funds can hold the same securities. Count underlying exposure, not tickers.

## Home-Country Weight

Canadian equities can align some assets with Canadian-dollar spending and receive specific taxable-account treatment. The Canadian market is also small globally and concentrated in several sectors. The appropriate weight depends on the complete balance sheet, costs, taxes, and the investor's policy.

Do not let familiarity substitute for diversification. Do not let a small tax advantage dominate portfolio risk.

## Write the Investment Policy

A useful policy includes:

1. Goal, amount, horizon, and withdrawal period.
2. Required liquidity and contribution schedule.
3. Target allocation and acceptable ranges.
4. Diversification and single-security limits.
5. Permitted investments and prohibited practices.
6. Rebalancing rule.
7. Account and tax constraints.
8. Valid reasons to change the plan.
9. Review frequency and decision owner.

Example: target 70% equities and 30% fixed income, direct new contributions to the underweight asset, and rebalance when either differs by more than five percentage points, subject to tax and trading cost. This is an example of a rule, not an allocation recommendation.

## Select the Implementation

Now evaluate ETFs, mutual funds, GICs, individual bonds, or other permitted investments. Review mandate, underlying holdings, fees, tax, liquidity, trading cost, and operational complexity.

A one-fund asset-allocation portfolio can simplify diversification and rebalancing. A multi-fund portfolio can provide more control. Added control is useful only when its measurable benefit exceeds maintenance and error risk.

## Rebalancing and Cash Flow

Assets drift as they earn different returns. Rebalancing restores intended risk; it is not market timing. Use contributions and withdrawals first to reduce trades. In taxable accounts, include realized gains and losses.

Set calendar reviews, percentage bands, or both. Narrow bands can create unnecessary trading. Wide bands can allow risk to change materially.

## When to Change the Plan

Strong reasons include a changed goal, shorter horizon, loss of income, new concentrated exposure, withdrawals beginning, tax-residence change, or evidence the original risk assessment was wrong. A product closure can require implementation change without changing the policy.

Weak reasons include recent outperformance, a forecast, a popular theme, or discomfort after a decline already contemplated by the plan.

## Common Mistakes

- Selecting products before the allocation.
- Investing short-term required money.
- Using recent returns as expected returns.
- Treating age as the only risk input.
- Counting funds instead of underlying holdings.
- Ignoring employment and property concentration.
- Writing no rebalancing rule.
- Increasing risk to repair a shortfall.
- Changing strategy during an ordinary market decline.

## Action Checklist

1. Define each goal and the consequence of loss.
2. Fund immediate and short-term liabilities separately.
3. Estimate return needs conservatively.
4. Choose allocation from capacity and tolerance.
5. Map total household concentration.
6. Write allocation, ranges, contributions, and rebalancing.
7. Select diversified products and calculate all-in cost.
8. Review annually and after structural changes.

## FAQ

### Is a 100% equity portfolio appropriate for a young investor?

Age alone is not enough. Income, debt, liquidity, near-term goals, horizon, and ability to remain invested matter.

### How many funds are needed for diversification?

The underlying exposure matters. One broad portfolio fund can be more diversified than several concentrated funds.

### Does diversification prevent losses?

No. It reduces specific concentration. Global equity and bond markets can still decline.

### How formal must the policy be?

A dated one-page policy can be sufficient if it contains clear rules and is used.
