## Executive Summary

Evaluate rental property as an operating business and leveraged investment. Use realistic rent, vacancy, non-payment, repairs, capital replacement, property tax, insurance, utilities, management, legal compliance, financing, transaction costs, and income tax. Appreciation is uncertain and should not be required to cover negative operations.

Provincial and municipal tenancy, licensing, rent, eviction, and short-term-rental rules vary. Obtain local legal and tax advice before purchasing.

## Build Net Operating Income

> Effective gross income = scheduled rent minus vacancy and credit loss plus other reliable income.

> Net operating income = effective gross income minus operating expenses, before mortgage payments and income tax.

Exclude mortgage principal and interest from NOI so properties can be compared independent of financing. Then subtract debt service to estimate pre-tax cash flow.

## Worked Example

Assume monthly rent of $3,000, or $36,000 annually. Apply 4% vacancy and credit loss, leaving $34,560. Property tax is $5,000, insurance $1,800, owner-paid utilities $1,200, maintenance $3,000, capital reserve $2,500, and management $2,765. NOI is about $18,295 before financing and tax.

If annual debt service is $24,000, cash flow is negative $5,705 before tax. Principal repayment builds equity, but the investor must fund the cash shortfall. Do not describe the property as cash-flow positive by omitting reserves or vacancy.

## Financing and Renewal Risk

Investor down-payment, qualification, insurance, and rate terms differ from owner-occupied housing. Test higher rates at renewal, months without rent, and a major repair. Leverage amplifies gains and losses.

## Tax Mechanics

Net rental income is generally taxable. Eligible current expenses may be deductible; capital expenditures and capital cost allowance follow different rules. Interest deductibility depends on use of borrowed funds and other facts. Claiming capital cost allowance can affect later recapture and principal-residence decisions.

Keep complete records and obtain advice. Tax treatment does not make an uneconomic property attractive.

## Management and Legal Risk

Landlord work includes screening, maintenance, notices, accounting, privacy, safety, and compliance. Professional management reduces work but not ownership responsibility and adds cost.

Model the property's use under current zoning and tenancy law. Do not assume a short-term-rental or secondary-suite strategy is lawful or durable.

## Concentration and Opportunity Cost

Compare the down payment, closing costs, and future capital calls with a diversified portfolio. Property is illiquid, locally concentrated, and expensive to transact. It can also offer tangible control and financing that public investments do not.

Include existing home and employment exposure. A second property can make most household wealth dependent on one regional housing market.

## Decision Framework

1. Verify lawful use and realistic market rent.
2. Calculate NOI with vacancy and capital reserves.
3. Add financing and stress a higher renewal rate.
4. Estimate tax and bookkeeping obligations.
5. Price management time or fees.
6. Compare with a diversified alternative using the same cash flows.
7. Test a sale after transaction costs and no appreciation.

## Common Mistakes

- Counting gross rent as return.
- Omitting vacancy, management, and capital replacement.
- Treating principal repayment as operating profit.
- Assuming appreciation will cover negative cash flow.
- Ignoring tenant and municipal rules.
- Underestimating tax records and sale costs.
- Adding housing concentration without measuring it.

## FAQ

### What cap rate is good?

No universal cap rate is sufficient. Risk, location, condition, tenancy rules, financing, growth assumptions, and alternatives matter.

### Is mortgage principal an expense?

It is a cash outflow and builds equity, but is excluded from NOI. Include it when evaluating household cash flow.

### Can losses reduce employment income?

Tax treatment depends on the facts and valid expenses. A reasonable expectation and compliant rental activity matter. Obtain tax advice for material losses.

## Return Measures

> Capitalization rate = net operating income ÷ purchase price or current value.

> Cash-on-cash return = annual pre-tax cash flow ÷ cash invested.

> Leveraged total return = cash flow + principal repaid + change in property value - transaction costs, divided by invested equity.

Cap rate ignores financing. Cash-on-cash can look attractive because leverage reduces initial equity while increasing downside. Total return depends on uncertain sale value. Show all three and do not count principal twice.

## Acquisition and Exit Costs

Include transfer tax, legal work, inspection, appraisal, financing fees, repairs, and setup vacancy. At sale, include brokerage, legal, mortgage discharge or penalty, renovation, vacancy, and tax. A short holding can be weak even when annual operations are positive.

Test a sale at the purchase price after five years. If appreciation is required to overcome costs and negative cash flow, the underwriting should say so.

## Vacancy and Capital Stress

One annual vacancy percentage can understate clustered risk. Model several consecutive months without rent plus a repair and higher renewal rate. Turnover can combine vacancy, cleaning, repairs, leasing cost, and legal rent restrictions.

Capital reserves should reflect property systems. A percentage of rent is only a starting point. Roof, envelope, plumbing, appliances, heating, and shared-building assessments have different schedules.

## Compare With Public Investments

Use the same initial cash and later contributions. A diversified portfolio is liquid and easier to rebalance but volatile. Property provides control and leverage but is concentrated and operational. Compare after-tax cash flow, time, and the consequences of a poor outcome. Identify leverage explicitly.
