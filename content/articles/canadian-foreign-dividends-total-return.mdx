## Executive Summary

Canadian equities can align part of a portfolio with domestic spending and may receive favourable dividend tax treatment in taxable accounts, but the market is a small, sector-concentrated share of global equities. Foreign investments improve geographic diversification while adding currency, withholding-tax, product, and reporting considerations.

Dividends are one component of total return. A dollar of dividend and a dollar of price appreciation both increase pre-tax wealth, but timing and tax differ. A high yield does not guarantee a higher or safer return.

## Home-Country Allocation

Canadian investors often hold more Canada than its global market weight. Reasons can include currency alignment, tax treatment, familiarity, and lower implementation friction. The cost is concentration in Canadian sectors and economic outcomes.

Choose the weight deliberately and include employment, pension, and property exposure. A worker in a Canadian resource company may already have significant domestic economic risk.

## Currency Exposure

Foreign asset returns in Canadian dollars combine local-market return and currency movement. The currency in which an ETF trades does not alone determine exposure. Currency hedging can reduce some movement but adds cost and tracking differences.

Currency can increase short-term volatility, but global spending and corporate revenues are also international. Use a policy rather than switching hedges based on exchange-rate forecasts.

## Dividend and Total Return Math

> Total return = income distributions + price change, including reinvestment where measured.

If a $100 security pays a $4 dividend and ends at $102, pre-tax total return is about 6%. If it pays no dividend and ends at $106, total return is also about 6%. The investor can create cash flow by selling units, subject to tax and transaction considerations.

Dividend cuts can accompany business stress. Companies that retain earnings can create value through reinvestment or destroy it. Yield alone says little about quality or expected return.

## Tax Differences

Eligible Canadian dividends use gross-up and tax-credit mechanisms in taxable accounts. Foreign dividends generally do not receive that credit and may face withholding. Capital gains are generally recognized on disposition, permitting deferral, while distributions can create current tax.

Registered accounts change Canadian tax treatment but may not recover foreign withholding. Account location should be evaluated after allocation and product fit.

## Strong Cases

Dividend focus can suit an investor who values a defined cash-distribution process and accepts sector tilts and tax effects. A broad total-return approach can provide better diversification and allows withdrawals through dividends plus selective sales.

Neither approach removes sequence risk or guarantees income. Retirement spending should be funded by the portfolio's complete return and risk plan.

## Common Mistakes

- Owning only Canada because dividends receive a credit.
- Treating dividends as free return separate from company value.
- Choosing high yield without examining concentration and sustainability.
- Assuming foreign currency always increases long-run risk.
- Letting small tax differences override diversification.
- Comparing yield with total return.

## Decision Framework

1. Set global and Canadian allocation based on total household exposure.
2. Identify currency and tax implications.
3. Compare total return, not yield alone.
4. Measure sector and company concentration.
5. Choose an account and product implementation.
6. Define how cash withdrawals will be generated.

## FAQ

### Are Canadian dividends tax-free?

No. They receive specific tax treatment in taxable accounts, which varies by dividend type, income, and province.

### Is selling shares to fund spending worse than receiving dividends?

Not inherently. Compare total return, tax, transaction cost, allocation, and sustainability. A sale can be part of a planned withdrawal strategy.

### Should currency be hedged?

There is no universal answer. Horizon, asset type, spending currency, cost, and preference for volatility matter.

## Account-Location Example

Suppose a household has Canadian equity, foreign equity, and bonds across TFSA, RRSP, and taxable accounts. Putting the highest-yield Canadian shares in taxable solely for the dividend credit may create excessive domestic concentration. Putting every foreign holding in an RRSP may be impractical if the TFSA is the only available room.

Start with the target allocation across the household. Then compare expected tax drag, foreign withholding, liquidity, and rebalancing. Preserve the allocation if a small tax saving would create meaningful risk or administration.

Directly held US-listed securities and Canadian-listed funds can receive different treaty and withholding treatment depending on account and structure. Currency conversion and trading cost can exceed the withholding difference on small balances. Verify current issuer tax information and obtain advice for material amounts.

## Withdrawal Design

A dividend-only spending rule can force an investor to hold more high-yield sectors than the risk policy permits. A total-return rule accepts distributions and sells selected holdings according to rebalancing and tax rules. Sales are not automatically capital gains equal to proceeds because adjusted cost base matters.

Income stability can also be misleading. Dividends can be reduced, and a portfolio with stable cash distributions can still lose market value. Fund essential spending through the complete retirement-income plan.
