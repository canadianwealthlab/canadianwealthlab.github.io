## Quick Answer

For an owner-occupied home, the current minimum down payment is generally 5% of a purchase price up to $500,000; 5% on the first $500,000 plus 10% on the portion from $500,000 to $1.5 million; and 20% at $1.5 million or more. A down payment below 20% normally requires mortgage loan insurance and its premium is usually added to the mortgage.

## Minimum Down Payment Formula

The price tiers are marginal, meaning the higher percentage applies only to the portion in that tier.

- At $500,000, the minimum is $25,000.
- At $800,000, it is $25,000 plus 10% of $300,000, or $55,000.
- At $1.2 million, it is $25,000 plus 10% of $700,000, or $95,000.
- At $1.5 million or more, the minimum is 20% of the full purchase price.

These examples describe general owner-occupied requirements. Rental properties, unusual properties, self-employed borrowers, and lender-specific situations can require more.

## What Mortgage Insurance Does

Mortgage loan insurance protects the lender if the borrower defaults; it does not protect the borrower. It is generally required when the down payment is below 20%. The premium depends on the loan-to-value ratio and can usually be added to the mortgage, increasing the amount on which interest is paid.

Provincial sales tax may apply to the insurance premium in some provinces and may need to be paid at closing rather than financed. Confirm the current insurer and provincial treatment with the lender.

## Minimum Versus 20% Down

Putting down less can get you into a home sooner and preserve liquid savings, but it produces a larger mortgage and an insurance premium. Reaching 20% avoids default-insurance premiums and reduces the loan, but waiting can expose you to price and rent changes and may consume cash needed elsewhere.

The decision should compare complete scenarios:

- Mortgage principal and payment.
- Insurance premium and interest on that premium.
- Savings left after closing.
- The opportunity cost of a larger down payment.
- How long you expect to own the home.

## Do Not Forget Closing Costs

A household with exactly the minimum down payment is not ready to close. Budget separately for legal work, inspection, appraisal, land transfer or property transfer tax, title insurance, moving, adjustments, and immediate repairs. First-time-buyer programs may reduce some costs but vary by province and eligibility.

Keep an emergency reserve after closing. Using every liquid dollar for the down payment can turn a routine repair into high-interest debt.

## Sources of a Down Payment

Savings and gifts are common, but lenders require documentation. Eligible buyers may also consider the Home Buyers’ Plan or a First Home Savings Account. These programs have eligibility, timing, contribution, withdrawal, and repayment rules. A program’s tax advantage does not make a purchase affordable by itself.

Avoid borrowing the down payment unless the lender explicitly accepts it and the resulting payment is included in your debt assessment.

## Decision Checklist

- Calculate the legal minimum for the exact purchase price.
- Obtain an insurance-premium estimate if below 20%.
- Reserve closing costs and emergency cash before setting the down payment.
- Compare monthly payments at several down-payment levels.
- Test higher renewal rates and a temporary income disruption.
- Verify current rules with the lender and mortgage insurer before offering.

## FAQ

### Do I need 20% down to buy a home in Canada?

Not always. Eligible owner-occupied purchases below $1.5 million can generally use an insured mortgage with less than 20% down, subject to insurer and lender approval.

### Can I add mortgage insurance to the loan?

The premium is commonly added to the mortgage principal. Any applicable provincial sales tax on the premium may have to be paid separately at closing.

### Is a bigger down payment always better?

No. It reduces the mortgage and may avoid insurance, but it can leave the household short of emergency cash or divert money from higher-priority debt and savings goals.

## Calculate the 2026 Federal Minimum

For an owner-occupied home priced at $500,000 or less, the general federal minimum is 5%. From $500,000 to under $1.5 million, it is 5% of the first $500,000 plus 10% of the remainder. At $1.5 million or more, the general minimum is 20%.

- $450,000 home: $22,500 minimum.
- $800,000 home: $25,000 plus $30,000, or $55,000.
- $1,499,000 home: $25,000 plus $99,900, or $124,900.
- $1,500,000 home: $300,000.

These are federal minimums, not lender approval. Lenders can require more based on property, income, credit, occupancy, source of funds, or eligibility.

## Mortgage Loan Insurance

A down payment below 20% normally requires mortgage default insurance. It protects the lender. The premium is based on loan-to-value and can often be added to the mortgage, increasing principal and interest. Provincial sales tax may apply and may be due at closing.

An insured mortgage can sometimes receive a lower rate than an uninsured mortgage. Compare rate, premium, loan amount, term, and total cost rather than assuming 20% always wins.

## FHSA, HBP, and TFSA

An eligible FHSA holder can contribute within personal room, generally subject to $8,000 annual and $40,000 lifetime limits. Contributions are generally deductible and qualifying withdrawals tax-free. Account opening starts participation-period rules.

The Home Buyers' Plan can allow an eligible RRSP withdrawal up to $60,000, with repayments or income inclusions over time. FHSA and HBP can be used together when conditions are met. The HBP removes assets from retirement savings and creates a repayment schedule.

TFSA withdrawals can fund a down payment. The amount returns as room the following calendar year, not immediately.

## Minimum Versus 20%

Minimum down payment preserves cash and may advance the purchase, but creates a larger mortgage and insurance premium. Twenty percent avoids the premium and reduces interest, but can delay buying or consume liquidity.

Compare additional rent while saving, uncertain home prices, premium, rate difference, interest, returns on retained cash, and reserves. No future value should be treated as certain.

## Source and Timing

Lenders verify the source and may require account histories, gift letters, sale agreements, or other evidence. Borrowed down payments have separate rules and add debt cost.

Plan transfers early. RRSP, FHSA, TFSA, investment sales, and deposits have settlement, form, and eligibility requirements.

## Complete Cash Checklist

1. Calculate the federal minimum.
2. Confirm lender and insurer requirements.
3. Verify account room and withdrawal eligibility.
4. Estimate premium and sales tax.
5. Budget transfer tax, legal, inspection, appraisal, adjustments, moving, and repairs.
6. Preserve a post-closing emergency reserve.
