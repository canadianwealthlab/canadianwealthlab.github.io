## Executive Summary

A personal money system converts income into four jobs: current spending, known future costs, resilience, and long-term goals. Use a small number of purpose-built accounts, monthly provisions for irregular expenses, and automation sized for an ordinary month. Review exceptions rather than every transaction.

The system should reveal the fixed-cost structure and the sustainable surplus. It should not moralize about spending or require perfect forecasts. A household with variable income needs a larger operating range and more conservative automation than one with stable salary income.

## Map Cash Flow Without Excessive Budgeting

Start with reliable net income. Separate spending into fixed commitments, flexible essentials, discretionary choices, and irregular costs. The distinctions matter more than dozens of merchant categories.

> Sustainable surplus = reliable net income minus fixed costs minus flexible spending minus irregular provisions.

Assume monthly net income of $8,000, fixed commitments of $4,000, flexible spending of $2,200, and annual irregular costs of $12,000. The irregular provision is $1,000 monthly, leaving an $800 sustainable surplus. Without the provision, the household might incorrectly automate $1,800 to investments and borrow when annual costs arrive.

Use two or three ordinary months to estimate flexible spending. A temporary low-spending month is not a sound baseline.

## Design the Account Structure

A workable default includes an operating account, an account for irregular planned costs, an emergency reserve, and investment accounts tied to long-term goals. Some households prefer separate goal accounts; others use one savings account with a ledger. Choose the method that makes assigned cash visible.

Avoid unnecessary fragmentation. Ten accounts can create reconciliation work, transfer delays, minimum-balance requirements, and idle cash. An additional account earns its place only if it prevents a meaningful error or supports a distinct time horizon.

## Understand Fixed-Cost Risk

Fixed costs cannot be reduced quickly. Measure them against reliable net income and test a lower-income scenario. A percentage rule is less useful than the actual ability to change commitments.

If fixed commitments are $5,000 and reliable net income is $8,000, a 20% income reduction leaves only $1,400 after fixed costs. That remainder must cover food, transportation, and every flexible or irregular need. The system may require a larger reserve or lower future commitments even if current cash flow is positive.

## Automate in the Right Order

Automate required bills and minimum debt payments first. Then transfer the monthly irregular-cost provision, reserve contribution, and sustainable long-term amount. Leave a defined operating buffer for timing differences.

Automation should respond to actual income. A variable-income household can automate a conservative base and allocate quarterly surplus after taxes and upcoming costs are reserved. A fixed transfer based on the best quarter can create recurring shortfalls.

## Plan for Irregular Expenses

Review the prior 12 to 24 months for property tax, vehicle maintenance, travel, gifts, professional dues, tuition, home repairs, and other non-monthly costs. Estimate the next annual amount and divide by the remaining months before payment.

An irregular expense is not an emergency merely because it is infrequent. A roof failure may be an emergency; annual property tax is not. Keeping them separate preserves the reserve for true disruptions.

## Build Feedback Without Constant Monitoring

Review four numbers monthly: operating cash, irregular-fund balance, expensive debt, and total amount directed to current goals. Review net worth and investments less often unless a decision is due.

Use alerts for low balances, large transactions, failed payments, and rate changes. The goal is to catch exceptions, not create continuous financial attention.

## Adapt the System to Variable Income

Base recurring commitments on conservative reliable income. When a bonus or commission arrives, reserve payroll or business tax obligations first, restore the operating and emergency ranges, fund known near-term costs, then allocate the remainder among debt and long-term goals.

Do not treat gross self-employment receipts as available household cash. Business expenses, GST or HST where applicable, income tax, and both employee and employer CPP contributions may need to be funded.

## Common Mistakes

- Automating from gross salary rather than cash received.
- Treating annual costs as surprises.
- Using too many accounts without a reconciliation method.
- Optimizing savings interest while carrying expensive revolving debt.
- Increasing commitments after a one-time bonus.
- Tracking every purchase while ignoring housing, debt, and vehicle commitments.
- Letting the emergency account become a general overflow account.

## Setup Checklist

1. Calculate reliable net income and four spending groups.
2. Convert irregular costs into a monthly provision.
3. Assign each account a single clear job.
4. Set required payments and conservative transfers.
5. Define operating and reserve ranges.
6. Create exception alerts.
7. Review after three months and remove complexity that does not help.

## FAQ

### Do I need a detailed budget?

Only if the detail changes a decision. Many households can control cash flow by tracking fixed costs, flexible totals, irregular provisions, and goal contributions.

### How much should stay in the operating account?

Enough to cover timing differences and routine volatility without overdraft. The amount depends on bill timing, income frequency, and the reliability of linked savings.

### Can one savings account cover several goals?

Yes, if a reliable ledger shows how much belongs to each goal. Separate accounts can be clearer but are not required.
