## Quick Answer

The Canada Pension Plan retirement pension is a monthly, taxable benefit based mainly on your contribution history, pensionable earnings, and the age you start. You can generally begin from age 60 to 70. The 2026 maximum at age 65 is $1,507.65 per month, but the average for new age-65 beneficiaries in April 2026 was $877.01.

## Who Qualifies

To qualify, you generally must be at least 60 and have made at least one valid CPP contribution. Quebec workers generally participate in the Quebec Pension Plan instead.

One contribution can establish eligibility, but it does not produce the maximum pension. The amount reflects how much and how long you contributed, your earnings, and applicable provisions that can exclude or credit certain periods.

## How Your Payment Is Calculated

Service Canada considers:

- The age when the pension starts.
- The amount and duration of CPP contributions.
- Average earnings through the contributory period.
- Applicable dropout or credit provisions.
- The CPP enhancement for eligible contribution years.

Retrieve a personal estimate through My Service Canada Account. Published maximums are useful context, not a forecast of your payment.

## Choosing a Start Age

Age 65 is the standard reference point, but not a required retirement age. Starting before 65 permanently reduces the monthly payment by 0.6% for each month early, to a 36% reduction at age 60. Starting after 65 increases it by 0.7% per month, to a 42% increase at age 70.

There is no increase for waiting past 70. The best age depends on longevity, health, cash needs, work, taxes, other income, and how delaying affects portfolio withdrawals.

## Working While Receiving CPP

Employment does not reduce the CPP retirement pension. If you work while receiving CPP and are under 70, additional contributions may create a Post-Retirement Benefit.

Contribution requirements can depend on age. Review the current rules if working between 65 and 70, including how an election to stop contributions may apply.

## Tax and Household Planning

CPP retirement benefits are taxable income. Tax is not always automatically withheld at the amount needed for your final return, so plan for instalments or voluntary withholding where appropriate.

Spouses or common-law partners may be able to share a portion of CPP retirement pensions for tax purposes if eligible. Pension sharing does not change the combined total paid and is different from credit splitting after separation or divorce.

## Applying and Checking Your Record

CPP does not always begin automatically. Apply in advance of the desired start date and verify the current process with Service Canada. Review your statement of contributions for missing or incorrect years early enough to investigate.

If applying after 65, limited retroactive payments may be available, but the rules do not make late application a substitute for planning.

## The Case for Starting Earlier

Starting before 65 provides income sooner and reduces the need to fund those years from work or personal assets. It is stronger when essential cash flow is limited, health or a shorter planning horizon reduces the value of delayed lifetime income, or waiting would force investment sales after a major decline.

Earlier CPP can also preserve accessible assets. That liquidity may have value even when a break-even calculation favours delaying.

The tradeoff is a permanently smaller monthly payment. Starting at 60 produces 36% less than the personal age-65 amount under current rules. The reduction continues for life and affects the inflation-adjusted base.

## The Case for Delaying

Delaying is stronger when the household has other resources, expects a long life, and values more lifetime income that does not depend on market returns. The 0.7% monthly increase after 65 reaches 42% at age 70.

Higher late-life CPP can reduce longevity and sequence risk. It can be especially valuable if cognitive, health, or investment-management capacity becomes more limited later.

The cost is larger portfolio withdrawals or continued work before CPP begins. If waiting requires selling depressed assets or paying high tax on RRSP withdrawals, the advantage can narrow.

## Break-Even Analysis and Its Limits

A basic break-even age compares cumulative payments.

> Break-even years = cumulative payments forgone while delaying ÷ annual increase in the delayed payment.

This is incomplete. Include inflation indexing, income tax, investment returns on earlier payments, returns and tax on assets spent while delaying, OAS recovery, survivor interactions, and the value of lifetime protected income.

Use several longevity and return sequences. A single precise break-even age can create false confidence.

## Worked Scenario

Suppose a personal age-65 estimate is $1,000 monthly. Starting at 60 would produce about $640 under current adjustment factors; starting at 70 would produce about $1,420. Before indexing and tax, annual amounts are $7,680, $12,000, and $17,040.

The age-60 choice pays for five years before the age-65 case begins. The age-70 case gives up ten years of the age-60 payment but later pays substantially more. The result depends on lifespan and what happens to the assets preserved or spent in each case.

Do not substitute the 2026 maximum for the personal $1,000 example. Obtain actual estimates for each start age.

## Coordinate CPP With the Whole Plan

Map employment, workplace pension, bridge benefit, RRSP or RRIF withdrawals, OAS, and spouse or partner income by year. Starting CPP in a high-income working year can create a different after-tax outcome from waiting, but cash-flow need may still control.

Delaying CPP while withdrawing RRSP funds can sometimes smooth tax and reduce later RRIF balances. It can also accelerate tax and deplete investments. Project lifetime after-tax cash flow rather than optimizing one program.

CPP sharing between eligible spouses or common-law partners, credit splitting after relationship breakdown, child-rearing provisions, disability periods, and survivor benefits can affect results. Verify personal eligibility and obtain advice for material interactions.

## Decision Framework

1. Retrieve personal estimates at 60, 65, and 70.
2. Verify the statement of contributions and relevant dropout provisions.
3. Build after-tax cash flow for each start age.
4. Test several lifespans and investment sequences.
5. Compare current liquidity with late-life income protection.
6. Coordinate OAS, pensions, RRSP or RRIF, TFSA, work, and household income.
7. Revisit before applying because cancellation options are limited and time-sensitive.

## FAQ

### What is the maximum CPP payment in 2026?

The maximum new retirement pension at age 65 is $1,507.65 per month for 2026. Most people receive less because the maximum requires a strong contribution history.

### Can I receive CPP and still work?

Yes. Working does not reduce the retirement pension, and eligible additional contributions can generate a Post-Retirement Benefit.

### Is CPP tax-free?

No. CPP retirement payments are taxable income.
