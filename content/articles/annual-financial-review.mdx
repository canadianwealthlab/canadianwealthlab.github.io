## Executive Summary

An annual review should identify material changes, not reward activity. Reconcile actual cash flow, update the balance sheet, verify account room, measure debt cost, review investments against written rules, and map decisions due over the next 12 months. End with no more than three actions and a date for the next review.

Use the review after tax filing or another consistent date when records are available. Add a separate event-driven review after a major change in income, household structure, housing, tax residence, or retirement timing.

## Reconcile the Year

Compare reliable income, total spending, taxes, debt repayment, and savings with the prior plan. Separate one-time events from structural changes. A bonus that increased savings should not be treated as a permanent monthly surplus. A recurring increase in housing or childcare costs should.

Calculate:

> Savings available for goals = net cash received minus consumption spending minus interest and fees.

Principal repayment increases net worth but reduces liquidity. Track it separately from new financial-asset contributions.

## Update the Balance Sheet

Record account and debt balances on the same date. Separate liquid cash, registered investments, taxable investments, property, and other assets. Separate short-term debt, long-term debt, and tax obligations.

Compare the change in net worth with contributions and market movement. If investments rose $30,000 but $25,000 came from new contributions, do not describe the full change as investment performance. If property value is estimated, use a conservative method and avoid letting an uncertain estimate drive spending decisions.

## Review Liquidity and Debt

Recalculate essential monthly expenses and the number of months covered by accessible reserves. Change the target if job stability, household income sources, housing, or fixed commitments changed.

List each debt's current rate, balance, renewal date, and prepayment options. A debt that was inexpensive last year may become the priority after a promotional rate or mortgage term ends. Confirm that extra payments are reducing principal and not simply offsetting new borrowing.

## Verify Registered Accounts

Reconcile personal records with CRA information for TFSA, RRSP, and FHSA room. Check direct transfers, withdrawals, employer contributions, pension adjustments, and unused deductions. Do not contribute solely because room exists. Confirm that the account fits the purpose and timeline.

Review whether an RRSP deduction should be claimed now or carried forward, recognizing that contributing and deducting are separate decisions. Consider current and plausible future marginal rates, but avoid assuming tax rates are known.

## Review Investments Against the Policy

Measure the current asset allocation and concentration. Rebalance only if outside the written range or if the goal or risk capacity changed. Compare all-in fees and verify that each holding still has a defined role.

Do not replace an investment merely because another performed better. Evaluate process, cost, diversification, tax consequences, and whether the original rationale still holds.

For taxable assets, update adjusted cost base for purchases, reinvested distributions, return of capital, and sales. Brokerage book value may not be sufficient for tax reporting.

## Review Housing and Major Commitments

Update mortgage rate, balance, renewal timing, and payment stress test. Review known repairs, condominium or strata assessments, property taxes, and major purchases. Fund predictable costs through a sinking fund rather than classifying each as an emergency.

If a move is possible, estimate transaction costs and how it changes commuting, childcare, and other cash flow.

## Refresh Retirement Planning

Update spending assumptions, retirement timing, CPP estimates, pension statements, asset balances, contribution expectations, and inflation. Focus on whether the required future savings changed materially rather than reacting to one year's market return.

As retirement approaches, build a year-by-year income and withdrawal map. Confirm which decisions need professional tax, pension, mortgage, or legal advice.

## Set the Next Three Actions

Rank actions by financial impact, deadline, and reversibility. Examples include paying an expiring high-rate balance, preparing for mortgage renewal, correcting a contribution-room discrepancy, simplifying an expensive holding, or increasing a reserve after an income change.

Record the decision, owner, deadline, and evidence required. A review that produces 20 unactioned tasks is less useful than one that resolves three important items.

## Common Mistakes

- Measuring progress only through investment returns.
- Updating market values but not interest rates or account room.
- Rebalancing without considering tax consequences.
- Treating every annual expense as an emergency.
- Refreshing article dates or assumptions without checking the source.
- Making changes because the review feels unproductive without a transaction.

## Review Checklist

1. Reconcile actual cash flow and unusual events.
2. Update the dated balance sheet.
3. Recalculate liquidity coverage.
4. Rank debt by current cost and deadline.
5. Verify contribution and deduction room.
6. Check allocation, concentration, fees, and taxable records.
7. Review housing commitments and major purchases.
8. Refresh retirement assumptions.
9. Choose up to three actions.
10. Schedule the next review.

## FAQ

### When should the review happen?

Choose a consistent date when statements and tax records are available. Tax filing season or year-end can work, but deadline-sensitive actions may require earlier checks.

### Should investments be reviewed more often?

Operational checks may be needed, but strategic changes generally do not require constant monitoring. Use alerts and written rebalancing rules.

### What if the year was unusual?

Separate one-time effects from recurring changes. Update the plan only where the underlying cash flow, risk, or goal changed.
