## Executive Summary

A sole proprietor reports net business income on a personal return and generally pays both employee and employer portions of CPP, except in Quebec where QPP applies. GST or HST registration can be required based on taxable supplies and special rules. An incorporated business files a corporate return, maintains a separate legal and accounting structure, and pays the owner through salary, dividends, or other valid transactions.

Incorporation does not automatically create tax savings. Deferral may arise when active business income qualifies for a lower corporate rate and funds remain in the company. If most earnings are withdrawn personally, integration aims to make the combined tax broadly comparable, though outcomes and timing differ. Legal cost, accounting, payroll, loss of personal credits or deductions, passive income, and personal-services-business rules matter.

## Sole Proprietor Mechanics

Report gross revenue and eligible business expenses, generally using Form T2125. Net income enters the personal return. Maintain invoices, receipts, mileage and home-office support where claimed, and records separating business from personal spending.

An expense must meet the applicable income-earning rules and may be limited, capitalized, or prorated. Calling an account “business” does not make personal spending deductible.

Self-employed individuals generally file by June 15, but balances owing remain due April 30 under current general rules. Late payment can therefore accrue interest before the filing deadline.

## GST or HST and Payroll

Registration is generally required after exceeding the small-supplier threshold for taxable supplies, with timing determined by how the threshold is crossed. Some activities have special rules, including commercial ride-sharing. Provincial sales taxes and Quebec administration can differ.

If employees are hired or salary is paid from a corporation, payroll registration, withholding, remittance, and reporting obligations can apply. Cash retained for remittances is not available personal income.

## What Incorporation Changes

A corporation is separate from the owner. It needs separate records, banking, contracts, filings, and governance. Corporate losses and assets belong to the company. Removing money without documenting salary, dividend, reimbursement, loan, or another valid treatment can create tax issues.

Liability protection is limited and fact-specific. Personal guarantees, professional obligations, director liability, and improper conduct can still create personal exposure. Obtain legal advice rather than treating incorporation as a complete shield.

## Salary Versus Dividends

Salary is generally deductible to the corporation, taxable employment income to the owner, creates RRSP earned income, and normally triggers CPP contributions and payroll obligations. Dividends are paid from after-tax corporate income, use gross-up and dividend-credit mechanisms, do not create RRSP room, and generally do not attract CPP.

The comparison requires corporate and personal tax, cash needs, RRSP strategy, CPP value, payroll cost, and province. A hybrid can be appropriate. Do not select dividends solely because the immediate withholding appears lower.

## Tax Deferral, Not Automatic Saving

If a corporation earns $200,000 and the owner needs nearly all of it personally, little remains to compound at corporate rates. If the owner needs $100,000 and can retain the rest, a deferral may fund business or investment assets. Future withdrawal creates personal tax.

Passive investment income can reduce access to the small business deduction when associated-group thresholds are exceeded. Corporate investing also has refundable-tax and account-record complexity. Compare with paying personal tax and investing through registered accounts.

## Personal Services Business Risk

A corporation may be a personal services business when the individual would reasonably be an employee of the client without the corporation, subject to detailed tests and exceptions. A PSB is not eligible for the small business deduction and faces restricted expenses and different tax treatment.

Contract wording alone does not determine status. Control, tools, profit opportunity, loss risk, integration, and other facts matter. Obtain advice when revenue is concentrated with one client and the working relationship resembles employment.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating gross receipts as spendable income.
- Missing GST or HST registration timing.
- Confusing the filing deadline with the payment deadline.
- Deducting personal costs without support.
- Mixing corporate and personal money.
- Assuming incorporation permanently eliminates tax.
- Choosing dividends without considering RRSP room and CPP.
- Ignoring personal-services-business exposure.

## Decision Framework

1. Estimate stable profit after valid expenses.
2. Estimate personal cash required each year.
3. Identify liability, contracting, financing, and succession needs outside tax.
4. Compare sole-proprietor tax and CPP with corporate plus personal tax.
5. Include legal, accounting, payroll, and annual administration costs.
6. Test whether earnings can remain in the company.
7. Review PSB and GST or HST status.
8. Obtain province-specific professional advice before implementation.

## FAQ

### At what income should I incorporate?

There is no universal threshold. Profit retained, province, business risk, client requirements, costs, and personal cash needs matter more than gross revenue alone.

### Does a corporation eliminate CPP?

Dividends generally do not create CPP contributions, while salary does. Avoiding contributions also reduces future CPP accrual. Model the complete tradeoff.

### Can I invest retained corporate earnings?

Generally yes, subject to corporate authority and tax rules, but passive income has complex tax and small-business-deduction interactions. Compare alternatives with qualified advice.
