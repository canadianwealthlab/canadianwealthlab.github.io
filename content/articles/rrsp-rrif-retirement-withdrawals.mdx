## Executive Summary

An RRSP generally must mature by the end of the year the holder turns 71, commonly through conversion to a RRIF or purchase of an annuity. RRIF minimum withdrawals begin the following year and are taxable. Withholding on excess withdrawals is only a prepayment of tax, not necessarily the final liability.

Tax-efficient withdrawal planning compares lifetime tax and benefit effects, not only the lowest tax this year. Intentional RRSP withdrawals before mandatory conversion can sometimes smooth income, but may accelerate tax unnecessarily. Model CPP, OAS, pensions, spouse or partner income, TFSA, taxable gains, and future RRIF minimums.

## RRIF Mechanics

The annual minimum is based on the account value at the start of the year and an age factor. The younger spouse or common-law partner's age can generally be elected when the RRIF is established to calculate a lower minimum. This election has timing rules and should be confirmed with the issuer.

No tax is normally withheld from the required minimum, but the withdrawal remains taxable. Plan cash for final tax or instalments.

## Why Withdrawal Order Is Not Universal

Using taxable assets first can defer RRSP tax but allow the RRSP to grow into larger later minimums. Drawing RRSP earlier can use lower brackets and create TFSA contribution cash, but loses tax-deferred growth and accelerates tax.

TFSA withdrawals do not generally add to taxable income and can manage a high-income year, but preserving TFSA assets can also provide long-term flexibility. Capital gains in taxable accounts can be timed to some extent but market and concentration risk may require sales.

## Worked Illustration

Assume a retiree has $700,000 in an RRSP at 65, modest taxable income before 70, and expects CPP, OAS, and a pension later. Compare no voluntary withdrawals with annual withdrawals that fill part of a lower bracket. Project the RRSP balance, tax paid, future RRIF minimums, OAS recovery, and after-tax assets to a range of ages.

The better outcome depends on return, longevity, future rates, spending, and survivor circumstances. A one-year tax saving is insufficient evidence.

## Pension Splitting and Credits

Eligible pension income may be allocated between spouses or partners for tax reporting within current rules, and the pension income credit can apply to qualifying amounts. Eligibility depends on income type and age. CPP sharing is a separate program. Verify current mechanics.

## OAS Recovery Tax

Large RRIF withdrawals can push individual net income above the recovery threshold. Avoiding every dollar of recovery is not necessarily the goal if a withdrawal improves lifetime tax or risk. Include it as a marginal cost.

## Minimums, Excess Withdrawals, and Cash Flow

Separate the required minimum from the amount actually needed for spending. A retiree can withdraw more than the minimum, but the excess is generally subject to withholding at source. Final tax is calculated on the return using all income and credits, so withholding can be too high or too low.

If the minimum exceeds spending, the after-tax remainder can be moved to a TFSA when room exists or invested in a taxable account. That transfer does not reverse the taxable RRIF withdrawal. Coordinate instalments, withholding, and cash reserves so an April balance does not force an unplanned sale.

## Survivor and Estate Scenario

Model the first year after one partner dies. Household expenses may decline less than income, pension splitting can end, benefit amounts can change, and one person's tax brackets must absorb future registered withdrawals. A qualifying spouse or partner can often receive registered assets on a tax-deferred basis when the rules are met, but the later withdrawals remain relevant.

Also test the tax that could arise if registered assets are included in income on the final return without a qualifying rollover. Estate and beneficiary rules are specific, so confirm designations and legal documents with qualified professionals.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating withholding as final tax.
- Deferring every RRSP dollar without projecting RRIF minimums.
- Withdrawing early solely to avoid future tax.
- Ignoring pension start dates and bridge endings.
- Using average tax instead of marginal effects.
- Omitting spouse or partner and survivor scenarios.
- Allowing tax optimization to create unsuitable investments.

## Decision Framework

1. Map income and spending by age.
2. Project RRSP or RRIF balances under return ranges.
3. Include minimum withdrawals and benefit thresholds.
4. Compare early, proportional, and deferred withdrawal cases.
5. Include TFSA and taxable-account tax.
6. Test one-person survivor income where relevant.
7. Use qualified tax planning for a material decision.

## FAQ

### Must an RRSP become a RRIF at 65?

No. The general maturity deadline is the end of the year the holder turns 71, though earlier conversion can be chosen.

### Is the RRIF minimum tax-free because no tax is withheld?

No. It is generally taxable income. Zero withholding does not mean zero final tax.

### Should RRSP withdrawals begin before CPP?

Sometimes, but not automatically. Model benefit timing, tax, longevity, account growth, and spending together.
