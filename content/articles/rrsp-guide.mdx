## Quick Answer

A Registered Retirement Savings Plan can defer tax: eligible contributions may reduce taxable income, investment growth is sheltered while inside the plan, and withdrawals are generally taxable. Its value depends on the tax rate when the deduction is claimed, the tax rate when money is withdrawn, and whether the tax refund is saved.

## Contribution Room and Deduction Room

New RRSP contribution room is generally based on 18% of the previous year’s earned income, up to the annual limit, adjusted for pension participation and other factors. Unused room carries forward. Your notice of assessment or CRA account provides the key personal figures.

A contribution and a deduction are related but not identical. You may contribute now and claim all or part of the deduction in a later year, subject to the rules. This can be useful when taxable income is expected to rise, but the tradeoff is delaying the tax benefit.

## Why the Tax Rate Matters

An RRSP is most effective when the deduction is claimed at a relatively high marginal rate and withdrawals occur at a lower effective rate. If the rates are the same, an RRSP can match the after-tax result of a TFSA when the refund is also invested.

Taxable withdrawals can also affect income-tested benefits and credits. Retirement planning should model the household’s total taxable income, not just the RRSP in isolation.

## Employer Plans and Matching

Employer matching can materially change the priority. Before choosing between personal accounts, understand the match formula, vesting or withdrawal restrictions, investment options, fees, and how the pension adjustment affects future RRSP room.

Leaving available matching dollars unclaimed is different from choosing between two otherwise similar tax shelters.

## Withdrawals Before Retirement

An RRSP is not legally locked until converted to a retirement-income vehicle, but ordinary withdrawals are generally taxable and the room is usually lost permanently. Financial institutions withhold tax at prescribed rates; that withholding is a prepayment, not necessarily the final tax bill.

The Home Buyers’ Plan and Lifelong Learning Plan can permit eligible withdrawals under specific repayment rules. Missed required repayments are generally included in income.

## Conversion and Retirement Income

An RRSP must eventually be matured, typically by the end of the year the annuitant turns 71 under current rules. Common choices include a Registered Retirement Income Fund, an annuity, or a taxable withdrawal. A RRIF requires minimum annual withdrawals, which are taxable.

The best drawdown sequence depends on pensions, CPP and OAS timing, taxable accounts, TFSAs, estate goals, and benefit clawbacks.

## Common Mistakes

- Contributing without checking available room.
- Spending the refund instead of treating it as part of the plan.
- Assuming withholding tax is the final tax.
- Using an RRSP for a short-term goal without understanding lost room.
- Ignoring employer matching or high plan fees.
- Maximizing the deduction without considering a future higher-income year.

## FAQ

### Is an RRSP contribution always tax-deductible?

Eligible contributions can generally be deducted up to your available deduction limit, but the deduction does not have to be claimed in the same year as the contribution.

### What happens if I withdraw from my RRSP?

An ordinary withdrawal is generally included in taxable income, withholding tax is applied, and the withdrawn amount normally does not create new contribution room.

### Should I max my RRSP before my TFSA?

Not automatically. Current and future tax rates, employer matching, flexibility, income-tested benefits, and the goal’s timeline determine the better sequence.

## Contribution Versus Deduction

Contributing and claiming the deduction are separate. A valid contribution can remain as an unused contribution and be deducted later, but contributing earlier solely to delay the deduction is not automatically best. A TFSA can sometimes provide tax-free growth and preserve RRSP room without creating future taxable withdrawals.

Example: a $10,000 deduction at a 30% combined marginal rate may reduce tax about $3,000. At 45%, about $4,500. Compare the benefit of waiting with the tax-deferred growth and flexibility forgone. Benefit interactions can change the actual result.

## Withdrawals and Withholding

Ordinary RRSP withdrawals are generally taxable and contribution room is not restored. The institution withholds at prescribed rates, but withholding is only a payment toward final tax. The withdrawal stacks with other annual income and can produce additional tax or benefit reductions.

The Home Buyers' Plan and Lifelong Learning Plan have separate eligibility, withdrawal, and repayment mechanics. A missed required repayment is generally included in income. Verify current limits and timing directly.

## Spousal RRSP and Overcontribution

A contribution to a spousal RRSP can use the contributor's room and deduction while the spouse or partner owns the plan. Attribution rules can tax withdrawals back to the contributor when contributions and withdrawals occur within the relevant period. This is not a simple income-splitting shortcut.

CRA rules provide a limited overcontribution cushion in some circumstances, but it is not additional deductible room and tax can apply. Use the personal deduction limit and pension-adjustment information.

## RRSP Review Checklist

1. Verify deduction room and unused contributions separately.
2. Estimate current deduction value and future withdrawal treatment.
3. Include the tax reduction in comparisons.
4. Review employer plan fees and matching.
5. Check withdrawal, HBP, LLP, and spousal attribution rules.
6. Plan conversion and later RRIF minimums before retirement.
