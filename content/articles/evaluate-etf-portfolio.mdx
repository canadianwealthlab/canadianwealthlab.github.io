## Executive Summary

Evaluate an ETF by the role it must perform, then examine mandate, index or process, holdings, asset allocation, currency exposure, fees, trading characteristics, distributions, tax structure, securities lending, and operational history. Similar past returns do not make funds interchangeable.

A one-fund portfolio can simplify diversification and rebalancing. A multi-fund portfolio can provide control over allocation, tax location, and product choice. The added control is useful only if the investor will maintain it and the expected benefit exceeds cost and error risk.

## Start With Mandate

State the exposure required before opening a product list. “Canadian equity,” “global all-cap equity,” “short government bonds,” and “balanced asset allocation” are different roles. Reject funds that do not fit even if fees or recent returns look attractive.

Read the prospectus, ETF facts, index methodology where applicable, and issuer documents. Marketing names can obscure leverage, covered calls, factor tilts, concentration, or limited diversification.

## Examine the Portfolio

Review number and weight of holdings, sector and country concentration, market-cap coverage, bond quality and duration, currency hedging, and allocation ranges. For an asset-allocation ETF, understand how the issuer rebalances and whether the home-country weight matches the plan.

Compare tracking difference, not only the management expense ratio. Trading spreads, withholding tax, portfolio turnover, and implementation can affect realized results.

## Fees and Trading

The management expense ratio is recurring. Commissions, bid-ask spread, currency conversion, and premiums or discounts affect transactions. A thinly traded ETF can still hold liquid underlying assets, but trade execution and market conditions matter.

Use limit orders where appropriate and avoid assuming the last quoted price is the executable value. Large or complex trades may require professional execution guidance.

## One-Fund Case

One-fund portfolios reduce rebalancing decisions, can automate allocation, and make behaviour easier. They are strong when the built-in allocation and exposures fit, the cost is reasonable, and no important account or tax constraint is sacrificed.

They are weaker when the investor needs a custom withdrawal structure, has substantial taxable assets with embedded gains, or can justify and maintain a different allocation.

## Multi-Fund Case

Multiple funds allow exact weights, selective rebalancing, and potentially lower product costs or tailored account location. They add trades, monitoring, drift, possible overlap, and temptation to change allocations based on recent returns.

Worked example: a four-fund portfolio saves 0.10% annually on $100,000, or about $100 in the first year before compounding. If extra currency conversion, commissions, cash drag, or one delayed rebalance costs more, the theoretical saving disappears. On a much larger balance, the same percentage can become material.

## Decision Framework

1. Define the portfolio role and target allocation.
2. Compare mandate, index, and holdings.
3. Calculate recurring and transaction costs.
4. Review tax and account fit.
5. Estimate the annual value of any multi-fund advantage.
6. Compare it with maintenance and error risk.
7. Choose the simplest implementation that meets the requirement.

## Common Mistakes

- Choosing by ticker familiarity or recent return.
- Comparing fees without comparing exposure.
- Owning multiple overlapping funds.
- Assuming currency of trading equals currency exposure.
- Ignoring bid-ask spread and conversion costs.
- Building a custom allocation without a rebalancing rule.
- Treating small allocation differences as certain performance advantages.

## FAQ

### Is the lowest-fee ETF always best?

No. Exposure, tracking, liquidity, tax, structure, and fit matter. Compare cost only among products that perform the required role.

### Does high trading volume prove an ETF is safe?

No. Volume affects trading, not the investment risk of the underlying assets.

### Is one fund enough?

It can be, when it provides the required diversified allocation. It is not automatically suitable for every account, tax situation, or withdrawal plan.

## Index and Tracking Review

Two funds labelled Canadian equity can follow different indexes. Compare eligibility rules, weighting, reconstitution, buffers, treatment of multiple share classes, and concentration caps. Market-cap weighting is transparent but can become concentrated in the largest securities. Equal-weight and factor indexes create different turnover and exposure.

Tracking difference is the fund return minus index return over a period. It can reflect fees, withholding, sampling, cash, trading, and securities-lending revenue. Tracking error measures variability around the index. A low MER does not guarantee the smallest tracking difference.

## Structure and Operations

Review whether the ETF holds securities directly or through other funds, uses derivatives, hedges currency, lends securities, or has material counterparty exposure. Read how collateral and lending revenue are handled. These features are not automatically good or bad, but must fit the role.

Check assets, market makers, spreads during normal and stressed conditions, creation and redemption mechanics, distribution policy, tax character, and closure procedures. A new fund may be sound but has less operational history.

## Evaluation Worksheet

Record the required role, benchmark, top holdings, geographic and sector weights, fixed-income duration and credit where relevant, MER, trading expense, tracking difference, spread, tax notes, currency exposure, and reason for selecting it. Revisit when the mandate or portfolio need changes, not because another ticker recently led performance.
