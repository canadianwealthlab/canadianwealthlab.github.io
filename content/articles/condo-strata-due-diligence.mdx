## Executive Summary

Buying a condominium or strata unit means acquiring a home and an interest in a shared corporation or association. Review financial statements, budgets, reserve or depreciation reports, insurance, meeting minutes, bylaws, litigation, fee history, engineering reports, and current or proposed special assessments. Requirements and document names vary by province.

Low monthly fees are not automatically good. They may reflect efficient operations, omitted services, deferred maintenance, or underfunding. Use qualified legal, inspection, insurance, and local professional advice.

## Financial Review

Compare actual operating results with budget. Look for arrears, one-time transfers, recurring deficits, rising insurance deductibles, and reliance on special assessments. Review the reserve balance against the latest study and scheduled projects.

Estimate the unit's share of known work and test a larger unexpected assessment. Lenders can also react to building condition or governance issues.

## Minutes and Governance

Read several years of council, board, and general-meeting minutes. Recurring leaks, envelope failures, elevator issues, disputes, and deferred votes can reveal risks that a current budget does not.

Review owner participation, management turnover, legal claims, and compliance orders. Silence in minutes is not proof that no issue exists.

## Insurance

Understand the corporation's policy, deductibles, exclusions, claims history, and owner responsibility. Obtain a unit policy matched to bylaws and provincial rules. A large shared deductible can become an owner exposure.

## Bylaws and Use

Confirm rental, pet, renovation, parking, storage, age, smoking, short-term accommodation, and move rules. Do not rely on listing statements. Verify whether parking and storage are titled, assigned, limited common property, or licensed.

## Physical Review

Inspect the unit and shared systems. A unit inspection may not cover roof, envelope, plumbing risers, parking structures, or mechanical systems. Review engineering reports and ask what work remains unfunded.

## Full Cost

> Condo ownership cost = mortgage + property tax + unit insurance + fees + utilities + maintenance inside the unit + assessment provision.

Include likely fee increases and a reserve for the owner's share of unexpected work.

## Common Mistakes

- Choosing the building with the lowest fees.
- Reading only the latest minutes.
- Assuming the reserve study guarantees funding.
- Ignoring insurance deductibles and exclusions.
- Relying on verbal statements about bylaws.
- Treating a renovated unit as evidence of sound shared systems.
- Waiving document review under time pressure.

## Checklist

1. Obtain all provincially relevant documents.
2. Review budgets, statements, arrears, and reserve study.
3. Read multiple years of minutes and reports.
4. Verify insurance and unit coverage.
5. Review bylaws, litigation, and assessments with a lawyer.
6. Inspect unit and shared-system evidence.
7. Stress-test fee and assessment increases.

## FAQ

### Is a large reserve always sufficient?

No. Compare it with building age, condition, scheduled projects, study assumptions, and unit entitlement.

### Can a buyer rely on the status certificate or provincial equivalent alone?

It is important but may not answer every physical or financial question. Review the complete package with qualified local help.

### Are special assessments predictable?

Some are signalled by studies and minutes; others arise unexpectedly. Build a margin and understand the building's history.

## Worked Financial Review

Assume a 100-unit building has $2 million in reserve and a study forecasting $6 million of work over ten years. The balance alone cannot be called strong or weak. Review project timing, planned contributions, construction inflation, unit entitlement, and whether assumptions remain current.

If a $3 million envelope project is required and only $1.5 million is available, the gap is $1.5 million before borrowing cost. An equal split would be $15,000 per unit, but actual allocation follows unit entitlement or provincial rules. Confirm approved, proposed, and reasonably foreseeable assessments and who pays at closing.

## Reconcile the Documents

Compare the status certificate or provincial equivalent, minutes, budget, statements, reserve study, engineering reports, and insurance. A budget may show no project while minutes discuss water intrusion. A study may assume a roof life that an inspection disputes. Ask the lawyer, inspector, manager, and council specific questions.

Review whether a recent fee increase merely caught up with underfunding. Stable fees can be a warning when insurance, labour, and repair costs rose.

## New and Small Buildings

New buildings may have limited operating history and developer-controlled budgets. Initial fees can omit realistic long-term maintenance. Small buildings spread large projects across fewer owners and may have informal governance. Neither is automatically unsuitable, but the margin for surprises differs.

## Provincial Differences

Document names, reserve rules, insurance, disclosures, and rescission rights vary by province. Ontario status certificates and British Columbia Form B information are examples, not national standards. Use local legal advice and current provincial sources.

## Questions to Resolve Before Removing Conditions

Ask which major projects are approved, proposed, or discussed; whether financing is arranged; how costs are allocated; and whether sellers or buyers are responsible at closing. Confirm responses against written records. If documents conflict or material information is missing, extend the review where possible or price the unresolved risk explicitly.

The decision is not whether the building is perfect. It is whether the purchase price, available cash, financing, insurance, and tolerance for shared governance fit the identified risks.
