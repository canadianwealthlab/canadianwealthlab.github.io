## Executive Summary

A financial roadmap is a sequence of decisions, not a single target. Start by making cash flow visible and protecting near-term obligations. Then address expensive debt, establish accessible reserves, use valuable employer programs, and match savings accounts and investments to specific goals. Housing, retirement, and tax choices enter when the foundation can support them.

The order is not fixed. A household with unstable income may need more liquidity before investing. A worker with an employer match may contribute enough to capture it while still paying expensive debt. A first-time home buyer who is eligible for an FHSA may prioritize that account before a TFSA or RRSP. The point of the roadmap is to identify the next bottleneck and revisit it when circumstances change.

## What the Roadmap Covers

This guide is for readers who have several competing priorities and need a way to organize them. It is not a personalized financial plan. Tax rates, benefits, account room, lending terms, and provincial rules depend on individual facts.

The roadmap has five stages: organize, stabilize, direct, build, and review. A household can work on more than one stage at once, but unresolved problems in an earlier stage can make later strategies fragile.

## Stage 1: Organize the System

Begin with a current balance sheet and a cash-flow snapshot. List every account, debt, recurring commitment, irregular annual cost, and financial deadline. Use after-tax cash received for cash-flow planning, and use current balances rather than estimates for debt and net worth.

The basic equations are:

> Monthly cash surplus = reliable net income minus regular spending minus monthly provisions for irregular costs.

> Net worth = financial assets plus property value minus all liabilities.

Cash flow answers whether the plan is sustainable now. Net worth shows the cumulative result. Neither measure alone is sufficient. A household can have a high net worth and weak liquidity, or a positive monthly surplus while carrying expensive debt.

Create a small number of functional accounts: one for incoming cash and routine bills, one for near-term reserves, and investment accounts tied to longer goals. More accounts are useful only when they make decisions easier. Automation should move money after income arrives, while leaving enough operating cash to avoid overdrafts or forced borrowing.

## Stage 2: Stabilize the Foundation

Stability means the plan can absorb an ordinary disruption without immediately relying on high-cost credit. The first target is often a starter reserve, not a fully funded emergency account. The appropriate amount depends on essential spending, job stability, household income sources, health, dependants, housing risk, and access to other liquid resources.

At the same time, make at least the required payments on every debt. Arrears, penalties, and damaged credit can be more urgent than optimizing returns. Expensive revolving debt normally deserves priority because paying it down produces a certain reduction in future interest expense.

Example: paying $5,000 against a credit card charging 20% avoids roughly $1,000 of annual interest if the balance would otherwise remain constant. An investment would need to earn more than 20% after fees and tax to match that first-year benefit, with no guarantee it will do so. A 4% mortgage creates a much closer decision because the interest saving is lower and the investment horizon may be long.

## Stage 3: Direct New Savings

Once immediate instability is controlled, assign each dollar to a purpose before selecting an account or investment. Money needed within a few years should usually emphasize liquidity and principal stability. Long-term money can accept more market variability if the household has the capacity and willingness to stay invested.

Check employer plans first. A matching contribution can materially change the comparison, but plan rules, vesting, fees, and investment options matter. Capture only benefits you understand and can use without undermining urgent obligations.

Then evaluate registered accounts. A TFSA contribution is not deductible, but qualifying growth and withdrawals are generally tax-free, and withdrawals create new contribution room in the following calendar year. An RRSP contribution can be deducted, growth is tax-deferred, and withdrawals are generally taxable. An FHSA can combine a deduction with a tax-free qualifying home withdrawal for an eligible first-time buyer. Account room and eligibility must be verified from personal records and current CRA guidance.

## Stage 4: Build Long-Term Capacity

Long-term wealth comes from the interaction of savings, time, investment returns, fees, taxes, and behaviour. The future value of a regular annual contribution can be estimated as:

> Future value = annual contribution × [((1 + return)^years - 1) ÷ return].

If a household invests $12,000 at the end of each year for 25 years and earns a hypothetical 5% after fees, the future value is about $573,000. At 3%, it is about $438,000. At 7%, it is about $759,000. These are scenarios, not forecasts, and the actual path will be uneven.

Housing belongs in this stage when the household can fund the down payment, transaction costs, ongoing ownership costs, and reserves without making the rest of the plan brittle. Retirement planning should estimate spending first, subtract expected reliable income such as CPP, OAS, and pensions, then determine what personal assets must fund.

## Stage 5: Review and Adapt

Review the system annually and after material changes. Update balances, contribution room, beneficiaries where relevant to the accounts you hold, debt rates, mortgage terms, planned purchases, tax assumptions, and retirement estimates. Compare the plan with actual behaviour rather than the budget you intended to follow.

A review should answer five questions:

1. Is cash flow consistently positive after irregular costs?
2. Is the liquidity reserve still appropriate for current risks?
3. Is expensive debt declining on schedule?
4. Are account and investment choices still matched to their goals?
5. Has any tax, benefit, mortgage, or family change altered the next priority?

## Common Mistakes

- Treating account selection as the plan instead of deciding what the money is for.
- Investing near-term money because the long-run expected return is higher.
- using gross income for spending decisions without accounting for deductions and tax.
- Counting a line of credit as equivalent to cash even though access can change.
- Maximizing an RRSP deduction without considering future taxable withdrawals or near-term liquidity.
- Buying a home at the lender maximum instead of a payment the household can sustain.
- Setting a retirement number without estimating spending and public benefits.
- Making the system so detailed that it cannot be maintained.

## Practical Next Steps

1. Build a one-page inventory of accounts, debts, recurring costs, and deadlines.
2. Calculate one month of reliable cash surplus using a provision for annual expenses.
3. Identify the single risk most likely to force expensive borrowing.
4. Choose one near-term priority and one long-term priority.
5. Read the [CWL Financial Order of Operations](/start-here/financial-order-of-operations) to compare competing uses for the next dollar.
6. Schedule a review date rather than waiting for the plan to feel finished.

## FAQ

### Do I need to complete every stage before investing?

No. Some priorities can run in parallel. An employer match, a long time horizon, or an expiring account opportunity may justify limited investing while building reserves or repaying debt. The tradeoff should be explicit.

### Is net worth the best measure of progress?

It is useful but incomplete. Liquidity, debt cost, savings rate, goal funding, and the durability of cash flow provide essential context.

### How often should the roadmap change?

Review it at least annually and after material changes to income, household structure, housing, debt, tax residence, or retirement timing.
