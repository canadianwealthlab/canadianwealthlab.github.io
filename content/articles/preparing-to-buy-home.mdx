## Executive Summary

Home-buying readiness is broader than qualifying for a mortgage. A durable plan needs a lawful down payment, closing and immediate repair reserves, a payment that fits actual cash flow, and enough liquidity left after closing. It should also survive a higher renewal rate, income disruption, and ordinary maintenance.

The federal minimum down payment is generally 5% up to $500,000, 5% on the first $500,000 plus 10% on the portion up to $1.5 million, and 20% at $1.5 million or more. A down payment below 20% normally requires mortgage loan insurance. Lender requirements and eligibility can be stricter.

## Separate Qualification From Affordability

Federally regulated lenders generally apply a mortgage stress test using the higher of 5.25% or the contract rate plus two percentage points. Qualification uses prescribed debt-service measures and lender assumptions. It is a ceiling under those rules, not evidence that the resulting payment fits every household priority.

Personal affordability begins with after-tax cash flow. Include mortgage payments, property tax, heating, utilities, home insurance, condominium or strata fees, maintenance, and transportation changes. Then preserve savings for emergencies, retirement, and other goals.

## Calculate the Cash Required

The down payment is only one closing requirement. Budget separately for land or property transfer tax, legal services, title insurance, inspection, appraisal, lender fees where applicable, adjustments, moving, immediate repairs, and provincial sales tax on mortgage insurance premiums where applicable.

Example: on a $700,000 home, the federal minimum down payment is $45,000: 5% of the first $500,000 ($25,000) plus 10% of the remaining $200,000 ($20,000). If the buyer has exactly $45,000, there is no cash for closing costs or a post-closing reserve. That household has met one minimum but is not financially ready.

## Compare Account Options

An eligible first-time buyer may use an FHSA for deductible contributions and tax-free qualifying withdrawals. The Home Buyers' Plan can permit an eligible RRSP withdrawal, currently up to $60,000, with repayment obligations under the program. The two programs can be used together when conditions are met.

Do not use an RRSP contribution and expected refund as if the refund were available before timing is confirmed. Do not withdraw from a TFSA and replace the money in the same year unless sufficient room remains. Verify personal account room and program conditions directly.

## Test the Ongoing Cost

Estimate the all-in monthly ownership cost:

> Ownership cash cost = mortgage payment + property tax + utilities + insurance + fees + maintenance provision.

Principal repayment builds equity, so it is not an economic expense in the same way as interest. It is still a cash requirement. For affordability, cash flow matters. For rent-versus-buy analysis, separate principal from unrecoverable costs.

Maintenance is uneven. A percentage of property value is only a rough planning shortcut. Review the building age, systems, condition, reserve-fund information for a condo or strata, and likely major replacements.

## Stress-Test the Mortgage

Test the payment at the expected contract rate and at a meaningfully higher renewal rate. Include shorter and longer amortization cases. A longer amortization lowers payment but usually increases total interest and keeps debt outstanding longer.

Also test:

- One income temporarily unavailable.
- A major repair soon after closing.
- Property taxes or fees rising faster than expected.
- A move within three to five years.
- A home-price decline when selling costs are included.

The plan need not be immune to every event, but the response should not depend on guaranteed appreciation or immediate refinancing.

## Decide How Much to Put Down

Putting down 20% can avoid mortgage default-insurance premiums and reduce the loan. Putting down less may preserve liquidity or allow an earlier purchase, but increases debt and normally adds an insurance premium. The better choice depends on purchase timing, available reserves, debt cost, and alternative uses for cash.

Do not empty all accessible accounts merely to reach 20%. Compare the premium avoided with the value of liquidity, additional rent while saving, and the risks of the specific property market. None of those future values is certain.

## Due Diligence Before an Offer

Review financing conditions, title, property disclosure, inspection findings, municipal information, and building documents appropriate to the province and property type. Condominium and strata buyers should examine budgets, reserve or depreciation reports, minutes, insurance, litigation, bylaws, fee history, and special assessments with qualified local help.

Rental-suite income should be treated cautiously. Confirm legality, realistic vacancy, expenses, tax implications, and whether the lender will recognize the income.

## Common Mistakes

- Shopping from the maximum pre-approval rather than a household cash-flow limit.
- Treating the down payment as the complete cash requirement.
- Counting every dollar in a registered account without checking withdrawal rules.
- Assuming a variable rate will remain lower or a fixed rate will always be safer.
- Ignoring transaction costs when the expected holding period is short.
- Treating principal repayment as both an expense and equity in the same comparison.
- Assuming appreciation will repair an overextended purchase.

## Readiness Checklist

1. Verify the lawful minimum down payment and lender source requirements.
2. Separate closing cash and post-closing reserves.
3. Calculate all-in ownership cash flow.
4. Test a higher renewal rate and income disruption.
5. Review FHSA, HBP, and TFSA mechanics with current personal room.
6. Compare renting and buying over a realistic holding period.
7. Obtain legal, lending, inspection, and tax help appropriate to the transaction.

## FAQ

### Does a pre-approval lock in affordability?

No. It is conditional and may use assumptions different from your actual expenses and goals. Property details, income verification, credit, rates, and lender policy can change the final decision.

### Is 20% always the best down payment?

No. It may avoid insurance and reduce interest, but can consume liquidity or delay a purchase. Compare the complete alternatives.

### Should a down payment be invested?

Only in assets consistent with the purchase horizon and the consequences of loss. A near-term required amount generally needs more stability than long-term retirement money.
