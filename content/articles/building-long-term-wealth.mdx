## Executive Summary

Long-term wealth is not a product. It is the cumulative result of saving, owning productive or interest-bearing assets, controlling fees and taxes, managing debt, and avoiding decisions that interrupt compounding. The plan should survive market declines, changing income, and competing life goals.

Start with a defined purpose and horizon. Choose an asset allocation based on capacity for loss, not recent returns. Use TFSA, RRSP, FHSA, workplace, and taxable accounts according to eligibility and tax context. Keep the portfolio diversified and simple enough to maintain.

## Define Wealth in Functional Terms

Net worth is useful, but the composition matters. Home equity cannot pay a near-term bill without a sale or borrowing. A large RRSP is taxable when withdrawn. A TFSA is more flexible but limited by contribution room. A taxable portfolio offers access but creates reporting and tax obligations.

Measure progress through several lenses: liquid reserves, expensive debt, investable assets, total net worth, goal funding, and required future contributions. Avoid treating one rising asset price as proof that the complete plan is improving.

## Use the Savings Rate as a Controllable Input

Returns are uncertain. Contributions and fees are more controllable. A useful planning equation is:

> Ending value = existing assets grown over time + future contributions grown over time.

Assume $100,000 is invested for 20 years with $12,000 added at each year-end. At a hypothetical 5% annual return after fees, the result is about $612,000. At 3%, it is about $462,000; at 7%, about $819,000. Taxes and the sequence of returns can alter the result. Use a range rather than a single forecast.

An increase in regular contributions can be more reliable than seeking a higher return through concentrated risk.

## Match Risk to the Goal

Risk tolerance is the emotional willingness to accept loss. Risk capacity is the financial ability to do so without changing the goal or selling. A long horizon increases capacity, but it does not eliminate the possibility of a severe decline.

Money needed within a few years should emphasize preservation and liquidity. Long-term goals can use more equities when the household can tolerate volatility. Asset allocation should be selected before individual funds.

## Diversify Deliberately

Diversification spreads exposure across companies, sectors, countries, currencies, and asset types. It cannot prevent all losses, but it reduces dependence on one security or economic outcome.

Canadian investors face a home-country concentration risk because Canadian markets represent only part of global equity markets and are concentrated in certain sectors. Foreign exposure adds currency and withholding-tax considerations, but can broaden diversification. The allocation should reflect the total household balance sheet, including employment and property exposure.

## Use Accounts for Tax Purpose, Not Status

A TFSA can hold cash, GICs, bonds, funds, and other qualified investments. An RRSP is not itself an investment. Decide the asset and the account separately.

Use current and expected future tax rates as inputs, while recognizing they are uncertain. RRSP deductions can be valuable at higher current marginal rates, but future withdrawals are taxable. TFSA withdrawals generally do not add to taxable income and create new room the following year. FHSA treatment is valuable only when eligibility and qualifying-withdrawal rules are met.

Taxable investing requires adjusted-cost-base records and may generate interest, dividends, foreign income, or capital gains. Tax efficiency should improve an otherwise sound investment, not justify an unsuitable one.

## Control Fees and Friction

Fees compound in the opposite direction of returns. Compare fund management expenses, advice fees, trading commissions, currency conversion, spreads, account fees, and tax costs.

Example: $250,000 growing for 25 years at 6% before fees becomes about $1.073 million. At 5% after an additional one percentage point of annual cost, it becomes about $847,000, a difference of roughly $226,000 before tax. The example assumes smooth returns and no contributions, but it shows why recurring costs matter.

Lower fees are not the only objective. Advice, planning, tax coordination, and behavioural support can have value. Evaluate whether the service received is worth the complete cost.

## Write the Behavioural Rules Before the Decline

An investment policy statement can specify the goal, horizon, target allocation, acceptable range, contribution process, rebalancing rule, and events that justify change. A market headline is not itself a reason.

During a decline, first verify that near-term cash needs remain covered. Then check whether the portfolio is within its permitted range and whether the original goal or risk capacity changed. Avoid converting temporary price movement into a permanent loss through an unplanned sale.

## Include Housing Without Double Counting

A principal residence can build equity and reduce future housing uncertainty, but it also creates concentration, financing, maintenance, and transaction costs. Do not assume the full property value is available to fund retirement unless the plan includes selling, downsizing, or borrowing.

When comparing mortgage prepayment with investing, use the after-tax mortgage interest saving, investment tax treatment, fees, liquidity, and risk over the same horizon.

## Common Mistakes

- Selecting securities before defining the goal and asset allocation.
- Treating recent returns as expected future returns.
- Counting gross investment returns while ignoring fees, tax, and inflation.
- Holding near-term money in volatile assets.
- Overweighting an employer, industry, country, or individual stock without measuring total exposure.
- Changing the plan because a simpler portfolio feels inactive.
- Assuming complexity improves expected outcomes.

## Action Checklist

1. Define each goal, amount, and time horizon.
2. Confirm the foundation can absorb near-term shocks.
3. Choose an asset allocation using loss capacity.
4. Select accounts based on room, tax, access, and purpose.
5. Choose diversified, understandable investments.
6. Calculate all-in recurring costs.
7. Write contribution and rebalancing rules.
8. Review annually, not continuously.

## FAQ

### Is home equity part of wealth?

Yes, but it is illiquid and tied to housing. Show it separately from financial assets available to fund other goals.

### Should I maximize every registered account first?

Not automatically. Account choice depends on eligibility, tax rates, access needs, employer benefits, and the goal. Contribution room is an opportunity, not an obligation.

### What return should I expect?

No precise return is knowable. Use conservative ranges tied to the asset allocation, subtract fees, account for inflation and tax where relevant, and test worse outcomes.
