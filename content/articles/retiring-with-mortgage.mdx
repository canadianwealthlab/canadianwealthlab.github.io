## Executive Summary

Retiring with a mortgage is not automatically a planning failure. The decision depends on payment size, rate and renewal risk, reliable retirement income, liquid reserves, tax on assets used for repayment, investment risk, housing plans, and preference for lower fixed obligations.

Paying off the mortgage offers a certain interest saving and lower required spending. Carrying it preserves assets and liquidity but exposes the household to rates and market outcomes. Model after-tax cash flow, not only net worth.

## Calculate the Retirement Burden

Record mortgage balance, payment, amortization, maturity, rate type, privileges, and penalty. Add property tax, utilities, maintenance, insurance, and fees.

> Debt-service share = annual mortgage payments ÷ reliable after-tax retirement income.

The percentage is a diagnostic, not a rule. A household with substantial liquid assets can tolerate a higher share than one relying entirely on fixed pension income.

## The Case for Paying It Off

Repayment removes interest, reduces renewal risk, and lowers required withdrawals. It is stronger when the rate is high, the payment strains income, the household values certainty, or the portfolio would otherwise need a high withdrawal rate.

It is weaker when repayment triggers large RRSP tax, realizes avoidable gains, consumes emergency cash, or leaves no diversified assets.

## The Case for Carrying It

Carrying the mortgage preserves liquidity and investment exposure. It is stronger with a manageable fixed payment, adequate reserves, long horizon, and a portfolio whose risk is acceptable.

Do not compare the mortgage rate with a gross expected return. Use after-fee, after-tax returns and test a severe market decline plus a higher renewal rate.

## Tax Cost of Repayment

Withdrawing $200,000 from an RRSP does not necessarily provide $200,000 after tax. The withdrawal can enter high brackets and affect OAS. A staged repayment or use of taxable and TFSA assets may produce a different result.

Tax should not prevent necessary risk reduction, but it should be modelled before a large withdrawal.

## Downsizing and Home Equity

Downsizing can release equity, but sale costs, purchase costs, taxes where applicable, moving, renovations, and market timing reduce the amount. Smaller homes are not always proportionally cheaper in the desired area.

Treat downsizing as a scenario, not a guaranteed rescue for an underfunded plan.

## Stress Scenarios

- Mortgage renewal two percentage points higher.
- Portfolio decline of 30% near retirement.
- One partner surviving on reduced household income.
- Major home repair.
- Move within the mortgage term.

Identify whether the response is spending reduction, asset sale, work, refinancing, or housing change.

## Compare Repayment Sources

List each possible repayment source separately. Cash and taxable investments may be available without creating ordinary income, although taxable sales can realize capital gains. TFSA withdrawals generally do not create taxable income and restore room in the next calendar year. RRSP or RRIF withdrawals are generally taxable and may affect income-tested benefits.

Do not assume that the account with the largest balance is the best source. Compare the tax, lost future growth, portfolio mix after withdrawal, and remaining emergency liquidity. If a taxable portfolio has a large embedded gain, calculate the tax rather than treating the market value as fully spendable.

## Partial Repayment and Renewal Planning

The choice is not limited to paying everything or carrying the existing balance unchanged. A permitted lump-sum prepayment can lower the renewal balance while preserving reserves. Increasing regular payments before retirement can test whether the future cash flow is comfortable. A shorter remaining amortization reduces total interest but raises required payments.

Confirm prepayment privileges, penalties, maturity dates, and lender procedures in writing. If retirement is close to renewal, compare terms while employment income is still available, but do not borrow more merely because qualification may later be harder.

## Common Mistakes

- Using gross retirement income for affordability.
- Withdrawing a large RRSP amount without tax modelling.
- Assuming investments will reliably outperform the mortgage.
- Emptying liquid reserves to become debt-free.
- Counting downsizing proceeds without transaction costs.
- Ignoring mortgage penalties and renewal timing.

## Decision Framework

1. Calculate complete housing cash flow.
2. Map reliable after-tax income.
3. Estimate tax on repayment sources.
4. Compare certain interest saving with net investment ranges.
5. Stress rates, markets, repairs, and survivor income.
6. Preserve necessary liquidity.
7. Consider partial prepayment or staged repayment.

## FAQ

### Must the mortgage be gone before retirement?

No. It must be affordable and integrated with reserves, income, investments, and risk.

### Is mortgage interest tax-deductible in retirement?

Interest on a principal-residence mortgage is generally not deductible. Deductibility depends on the use of borrowed funds, not the collateral, and requires specific tax analysis.

### Should CPP be started early to make payments?

Not automatically. Compare the lower lifetime CPP payment with the value of earlier cash and other available assets.

Revisit the decision before each renewal and after any material change in rates, income, spending, health, or housing plans.
