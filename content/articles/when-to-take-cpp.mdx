## Quick Answer

Starting CPP early provides more payment years but a permanently smaller monthly benefit. Delaying provides fewer initial payments but a larger inflation-indexed lifetime amount. The right choice depends on health, expected longevity, immediate cash needs, work, taxes, other guaranteed income, and the risk of drawing investments while waiting.

## The Permanent Adjustments

CPP uses age 65 as the reference. Starting before 65 reduces the payment by 0.6% for each month early, or 7.2% per year, to a maximum 36% reduction at age 60. Delaying after 65 increases it by 0.7% per month, or 8.4% per year, to 42% at age 70.

There is no financial advantage to delaying after 70. These percentages apply to your own age-65 amount, not automatically to the published maximum.

## Reasons to Consider Starting Earlier

- You need income for essential spending and have limited alternatives.
- Health or family longevity suggests a shorter planning horizon.
- Continuing to delay would require selling investments after a major decline.
- You value earlier cash flow more than higher future guaranteed income.

Starting early solely because “you might not live long enough” ignores taxes, survivor rules, investment risk, and the value of protected late-life income.

## Reasons to Consider Delaying

- You have other resources to fund the gap.
- You expect a long life and value higher lifetime protected income.
- You are still working and do not need the benefit.
- Delaying helps manage longevity and market-sequence risk.
- Current taxable income is high relative to expected later income.

Delaying is not automatically best if it forces unsustainable withdrawals or leaves the household short of liquid assets.

## Look Beyond a Simple Break-Even Age

A break-even calculation compares cumulative nominal payments and can be useful, but it is incomplete. A stronger comparison includes:

- Inflation indexing.
- Tax on CPP and on the funds used while delaying.
- Investment returns and the risk of those returns.
- The value of stable income at older ages.
- Survivor and estate objectives.
- Effects on OAS or other income-tested benefits.

Use multiple longevity and return cases rather than one precise forecast.

## Coordinate With Work and Other Income

CPP can be collected while working. Additional contributions may create a Post-Retirement Benefit if you meet the rules. Starting CPP in a high-income working year may produce a less attractive after-tax result than delaying, but personal cash flow can still justify it.

Coordinate the decision with a workplace pension, RRSP or RRIF withdrawals, OAS timing, and a spouse’s income. Household sequencing matters more than optimizing one benefit alone.

## A Decision Framework

- Obtain your personal age-60, age-65, and age-70 estimates.
- Build cash-flow plans for each start age.
- Test several lifespans and investment-return sequences.
- Compare after-tax household income, not gross CPP alone.
- Identify which risks matter most: running short now, market losses, or outliving assets.
- Revisit the choice before applying; once started, cancellation options are limited and time-sensitive.

## FAQ

### Is age 70 always the best time to take CPP?

No. Delaying can be valuable for longevity protection, but health, liquidity, taxes, work, and the need to draw investments can support an earlier start.

### What is the reduction if I start CPP at 60?

The monthly amount is 36% below the age-65 amount under current rules.

### What is the increase if I start CPP at 70?

The monthly amount is 42% above the age-65 amount under current rules.
